\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
Many financial quantitative risk valuation methods rely on accurate interpolation of implied volatility surfaces, yet traditional non-parametric models often struggle to provide this precision due to the microstructure noise inherent to financial data. While Gaussian Process regression offers a highly flexible modelling solution, standard deterministic point estimation techniques are highly susceptible to this noise contamination, resulting in weakly identifiable log-marginal likelihood landscapes. In response, the model overfits the microstructural market frictions (such as bid-ask spreads) by learning the surface and artificially inflating the OOS RMSE indicator. The result is a jagged implied volatility surface that violates strict static arbitrage constraints, including calendar and butterfly conditions.

The primary innovation of this research lies in framing Bayesian hyperparameter marginalisation as a structural regulariser, naturally filtering out the high-frequency overfitting that causes arbitrage violations in the first place. This approach fundamentally prevents the emergence of economic arbitrage violations without the necessity to artificially restrict the surface.

\section*{I. Basic Option Definitions}

\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{I. Basic Option Mechanics}
\label{sec:glossary_basic_option_definitions}
These terms define the fundamental building blocks of the derivative contracts modelled in this text.

\begin{description}
    \item[Option Contract:] A financial derivative that gives the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an underlying asset at a specific price on or before a certain date.
    \item[European-style Option:] An option contract that can only be exercised at the expiration date.
    \item[American Option:] In contrast to the European Option, an American option contract allows the holder to exercise their right at any time prior to expiration.
    \item[Call Option:] A contract whose payoff is tied to the right to buy the underlying asset, defined mathematically at maturity as $(S_T - K)^+ = \max(S_T - K, 0)$. Intuitively, an investor might buy a call to speculate if he thinks the price of the asset will go up; if that happens, he will be able to purchase the asset at the Strike Price $K$.
    \item[Strike Price ($K$):] The predetermined, fixed price at which the option contract can be executed.
    \item[Spot Price ($S_0$):] Current or initial market price.
    \item[Maturity / Time to Maturity ($T$):] The annualised time remaining until the option contract expires.
    \item[Premium:] The absolute market price of the option today, reflecting the compensation traders demand for bearing risk.
    \item[SPX:] The ticker symbol for S\&P 500 Index options, which track the largest Fortune 500 companies and serve as the highly liquid, primary benchmark for global equity derivative markets.
    \item[Bid-Ask Spread / Microstructural Noise:] The small-scale market frictions and discrete tick sizes present in real-world trading that introduce noise into observed option prices.
\end{description}

\section{Context and Motivation}
Accurate modelling of the implied volatility surface is central to modern quantitative finance. Volatility surfaces are required for pricing, hedging, calibration, and risk management, particularly in index option markets such as SPX. At the same time, Bayesian nonparametric methods, and Gaussian Processes in particular, offer a flexible framework for modelling nonlinear structure with uncertainty quantification.

This dissertation is motivated by the need for a reliable implied-volatility surface for pricing and risk management. As financial data is naturally messy, Gaussian Process regression is a flexible yet reliable solution that has the potential to interpolate this sparse and noisy volatility data smoothly. However, the practical performance of the regression is heavily dependent on the estimation of unstable kernel hyperparameters. This paper studies whether the Bayesian approach to those hyperparameters gives a more reliable surface.

\section{Problem Statement}

The problem considered in this dissertation is the failure of the deterministic point estimation algorithm in weakly identifiable topological landscapes of the Log-Marginal Likelihood for Gaussian Process volatility surface models. Under noisy and chaotic market data, the L-BFGS-B algorithm suffers from arbitrary hyperparameter configurations, depending on the initialisation coordinate. Because the ridge at the bottom of the LML surface is flat, any point yields near-equivalent log-marginal likelihood values but results in different predictive surfaces and pricing outputs.

\section{Research Questions and Hypothesis}
\label{sec:hypothesis}
\textbf{We can break up the objective into two main research questions:}
\begin{quote}
\begin{itemize}
	\item How does the weak identifiability of Gaussian Process hyperparameters impact the out-of-sample predictive accuracy of the implied volatility surface?

	\item Can Bayesian inference via Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC/NUTS) resolve this instability to improve pricing performance while maintaining arbitrage-free constraints?
\end{itemize}
\end{quote}
In this dissertation, we adopt the central hypothesis that flat ridges in the log-marginal likelihood topology cause deterministic optimisers to fail, and that replacing point estimation with a full posterior distribution over hyperparameters will enforce structural regularization, drastically reducing localized butterfly and calendar arbitrage violations, with only a minimal and acceptable trade-off in nominal out-of-sample prediction accuracy.

Although the individual mathematical and financial components of this hypothesis have been explored separately in recent literature, their synthesis represents a novel contribution to the modelling of implied volatility. Within the topic of machine learning, the pathology of weakly identifiable Log-Marginal Likelihood (LML) topologies is well-documented and researched; papers like (\citet{titsias_nd}) have specifically outlined the shortcomings of the point estimation method. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) is frequently proposed as an elegant solution to resolving the overfitting inherent in the deterministic optimiser.

Additionally, Gaussian Processes have been explored for volatility surface interpolation in recent quantitative finance literature. (\citet{cousin2021gp}) and (\citet{chataigner2021beyond}) have recognised that GP is a powerful non-parametric tool for surface interpolation. However, existing finance literature has often artificially specified strict arbitrage violations by relying on constrained kriging (See Dictionary) to explicitly avoid calendar and butterfly arbitrage violations. This thesis, on the other hand, seeks to bridge these two independent domains by establishing a direct causal link. Rather than treating arbitrage violation as a purely financial constraint that we need to artificially enforce. This research identifies the LML flat-ridge pathology and the resulting memorisation of microstructural noise as the fundamental mathematical cause of these economic violations.

To summarise, the novel contribution of this work lies in framing Bayesian hyperparameter marginalisation as a structural regulariser, naturally filtering out the high-frequency overfitting that causes arbitrage violations in the first place.

\section{The Implied Volatility Surface}
Before discussing the financial constraints, we should introduce the object to which they will apply: the implied volatility surface. The surface is a three-dimensional graph that maps the volatility of option contracts across several maturities and strikes (see \hyperref[sec:glossary_basic_option_definitions]{I. Basic Option Mechanics}) for a single asset. Specifically, a function mapping of an option’s strike $K$ and time to maturity $T$ can be defined as $(K, T) \mapsto \sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}(K,T)$ across available strikes and maturities. The annualised volatility figure is derived from the inverted Black-Scholes-Merton formula (see \hyperref[sec:glossary_volatility_surface_metrics]{II. Volatility \& Surface Metrics}) to standardise absolute option prices. This standardisation enables comparison of an option across different strikes and maturities.

Importantly, the Black-Scholes implied volatility (denoted as $\sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}(K,T)$) (see \hyperref[sec:glossary_volatility_surface_metrics]{II. Volatility \& Surface Metrics}) is the unique volatility parameter that, when used in the B-S model, yields the option’s exact market price. It acts as a forward-looking metric, pricing in the market consensus on expected risk and uncertainty rather than past price movements. In practice, it reflects the premium or compensation that traders demand today for bearing risk. This is because longer maturity dates of an option inherently carry larger variance, meaning that the price of an asset has more time to rise above the strike, which carries additional risk for the writer of the option.

This expected risk is difficult to evaluate by only looking at absolute metrics such as spot (see \hyperref[sec:glossary_basic_option_definitions]{I. Basic Option Mechanics}) and option prices. Hence, we should take a more relative approach in order to gauge this uncertainty, which implies constructing log-moneyness, defined as $k = \log(K/F_0)$ (see \hyperref[sec:glossary_volatility_surface_metrics]{II. Volatility \& Surface Metrics}). By using logarithms, we produce a stable and normalised representation of the volatility surface, independent of the underlying asset’s price dynamics.

\paragraph{The Role of SPX Options.}
For practical purposes, the underlying asset for this empirical evaluation is the S\&P 500 Index (SPX), which tracks the performance of the largest U.S. Fortune 500 companies. The SPX derivatives serve as the primary benchmark for global equity derivative markets. This is due to a couple of reasons: the SPX being exceptionally liquid across many strikes and maturities, possessing a European-style contract type (see \hyperref[sec:glossary_basic_option_definitions]{I. Basic Option Mechanics}) and abundant documentation. Additionally, the SPX surface possesses a highly pronounced structural downside skew. This extreme asymmetry makes SPX options a highly interesting testing ground for evaluating Bayesian hyperparameter inference and surface modelling.

\section{Static Arbitrage Constraints}
\label{subsec:static-arbitrage}
Before introducing the Gaussian Process model, we must specify the constraints that will ensure the implied volatility surface is not only mathematically, but also financially and legally sound. The two static arbitrage constraints that are of most importance for this purpose are the \emph{calendar arbitrage} and the \emph{butterfly arbitrage} (see \hyperref[sec:glossary_arbitrage_pricing_theory]{III. Arbitrage \& Pricing Theory}). These constraints allow for the surface to have a valid risk-neutral probability density function. In turn, this makes it financially impossible if it implies the existence of a portfolio with a non-negative payoff and a negative cost. Hence, a mathematically smooth and stable interpolant is not sufficient; it must also be economically admissible.

Another important topic of discussion that formalises the aforementioned is a framework central to asset pricing: the risk-neutral principle (see \hyperref[sec:glossary_arbitrage_pricing_theory]{III. Arbitrage \& Pricing Theory}). Intuitively, we use the risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$ in derivatives pricing as an equivalent probability measure to the physical measure $\mathbb{P}$, defined by the Radon-Nikodym derivative $\frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}$. It reweights the real-world probability distribution by shifting the expected asset return to the risk-free rate $r$. This parameter transformation absorbs investor risk aversion into the probability density itself, allowing us to compute no-arbitrage derivative prices as simple discounted expectations $\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[e^{-rT} X_T]$. We 

\begin{proposition}[Arbitrage-free call price under risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$]

Strictly, if $X_T$ is a payoff at maturity $T$, then its time-$0$ value is

\[X_0 = e^{-rT}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_T],\]

where $r$ is the continuously compounded risk-free rate and $\mathbb{Q}$ is the risk-neutral measure. For a European call option with strike $K$, the payoff is

\[(S_T - K)^+ = \max(S_T - K, 0),\]

so its arbitrage-free price is

\[C(K,T) = e^{-rT}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[(S_T - K)^+].\]

This pricing identity is the starting point for both static-arbitrage conditions: calendar arbitrage is linked to the option price rising over time, while butterfly arbitrage is linked to the payoff profile having a non-negative probability density.
\end{proposition}

However, these conditions are difficult to implement through pure implied volatility (\(\sigma _{BS}\)), which makes static arbitrage constraints highly non-linear and mathematically intractable. A paper on SVI Surfaces (\citet{gatheral2014arbitrage}) pioneered the idea of shifting to total variance space \(w(k, \tau)\), which linearises the no-arbitrage conditions, making them far easier to enforce mathematically. The relationship of implied volatility to total variance is straightforward:

\[w(k,T) = \sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}^2(k,T)\,T\]

where $k = \log(K/S_0)$ is log-moneyness. We prefer the total variance space for two reasons. Firstly, it removes the trivial $\sqrt{T}$ scaling present in implied volatility, thereby making IV surfaces across different maturities comparable on a uniform scale. The trivial proof will not be discussed here. Secondly, the calendar arbitrage conditions can be naturally expressed as monotonicity conditions on $w(k,T)$, while butterfly arbitrage conditions can be checked after converting the fitted surface back into call prices.

In other words, the total variance space serves as a smooth framework that allows us to conveniently assert the financial constraints, which we will discuss further.

%--------------------------------------------------------- calendar arb
\subsection{Calendar Arbitrage}
\label{subsubsec:calendar-arbitrage}

As described by Gatheral, calendar arbitrage arises from the calendar spread option strategy, which involves two options with the same strike price but different maturities. An arbitrage opportunity arises when the longer-maturity option has a lower total variance than the shorter-maturity one. This makes the near-month contract overpriced, which in turn could be shorted, and the far-month contract underpriced. It goes without saying that this applies only to European options or non-dividend-paying American options, which we will assume from now on. In practical terms, the total variance should increase monotonically with respect to time maturity \(T\).\citep{gatheral2006volatility}

\paragraph{Link to total variance.}
In total variance terms, the calendar arbitrage constraint becomes a monotonicity condition in maturity:
\[\frac{\partial w(k,T)}{\partial T} \geq 0.\]
The $w(k,T)$ represents the cumulative variance up to time $T$, the constraint implies that with every step, the variance should not decrease. In other words, the uncertainty should accumulate over time and never decrease; otherwise, it would create an arbitrage opportunity.

\paragraph{Numerical implementation.}
Numerically implementing the calendar arbitrage constraint in total variance space is also easier. We check the condition on the discrete maturity grid $T_1, \dots, T_n$ using first differences. 

\[\frac{w(k_i,T_{j+1}) - w(k_i,T_j)}{T_{j+1} - T_j} < 0\]

This provides a simple, transparent numerical proxy for the continuous condition $\partial w/\partial T \geq 0$.

\begin{proposition}[Calendar monotonicity]
For each fixed strike \(K\), the map \(T\mapsto C(K,T)\) is nondecreasing. Consequently, the total implied variance

\[w(k,T)=\sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}^2(k,T)\,T\]

is nondecreasing in \(T\) for each fixed log-moneyness \(k\); in particular, if \(w\) is differentiable,
\[\frac{\partial w}{\partial T}(k,T) \geq 0.\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let \(0<T_1<T_2\). No-arbitrage implies \(C(K,T_2)\ge C(K,T_1)\). Since the Black--Scholes call price is strictly increasing in total variance, the result is as follows.
\end{proof}

%--------------------------------------------------------- butterfly arb
\subsection{Butterfly Arbitrage}
\label{subsubsec:butterfly-arbitrage}

Butterfly arbitrage originates from the butterfly spread option strategy, which involves four options with staggered strike prices $K_3 > K_2 > K_1$. It entails purchasing a call at a low strike price $K_1$, selling two calls at a middle strike price $K_2$, and buying a call at a high strike price $K_3$ (where $K_3 - K_2 = K_2 - K_1$). This arbitrage indicates that the implied probability distribution of the asset is negative and invalid, often due to an overly steep or improperly smoothed IV surface during interpolation. Consequently, the position's payoff can become negative, meaning the trader profits risk-free without owning the underlying asset.

\paragraph{Intuition.}
A natural way to test this constraint is to examine the local curvature; the curve should always be convex, so the middle strike should not lie above the average of the neighbouring strikes. In other words, if the middle option price is too high relative to the neighbouring points, an arbitrage opportunity exists. The connection to the fundamental Breeden--Litzenberger theorem can be found in Appendix \ref{sec:breeden_litz_proof} for proof is that a sufficiently tight butterfly spread is a finite-difference estimate of the second derivative of the call price curve with respect to strike $K$. It is important to note that in the case of a butterfly arbitrage violation (implying probabilities), the local volatility in Dupire's framework becomes mathematically undefined, we direct the reader to Appendix B.5 for the full breakdown. This criteria is paramount for a valid volatility surface.

\paragraph{Relationship to total variance.}
As we proved, butterfly arbitrage can be naturally stated in terms of call prices, unlike calendar arbitrage, which is inherently a monotonicity condition on the total variance across maturities. However, since the fitted model is expressed in the total-variance space, the implied total variance space must be converted back to call prices before assessing the convexity condition. Simply, this is done using the Black--Scholes formula:

$$ C_{\mathrm{BS}}(S,K,T,r,\sigma) = S\,N(d_1) - K e^{-rT}N(d_2),$$

where

$$d_1 = \frac{\log(S/K) + (r + \tfrac12\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}. $$

\paragraph{Numerical implementation.}
The numerical implementation of the butterfly arbitrage mirrors the mathematical definition. We use the Breeden-Litzenberger condition, where the risk-neutral probability equals the second partial derivative with respect to strike $\partial^2 C/\partial K^2\geq 0$. This is done by implementing the central finite-difference approximation of option prices across a grid to detect negative probabilities, which would then be counted as a butterfly arbitrage. 

\section{The Parametric Prior}
In Bayesian nonparametric machine learning models like Gaussian Processes, setting a mean function lets us include financial knowledge before seeing any data. Rather than making the Gaussian Process learn the overall shape of the implied volatility surface from nothing, we use a parametric prior.
We define the prior distribution as:

\begin{equation}
w(K,T) = T\left(|a| + |b|(\log K)^2 + c \log K\right).
\label{parametric_prior}
\end{equation}

The mean function uses a quadratic polynomial in log-moneyness, which is heavily based on the Stochastic Volatility-Inspired (SVI) parametrisation (\citet{gatheral2014arbitrage}). By avoiding the full five-parameter SVI, which is difficult to optimise, the resulting model is essentially a parabolic curve in log-moneyness. Where $(\log K)^2$ provides the characteristic convex smile, $\log K$ is the volatility skew, and $T$ scales it across maturities.

Originally, the standard Gatheral's raw SVI formula is indifferent to the definition of variance, which can never be negative. Hence, the inclusion of absolute values for $a$ and $b$. Alternatively, a standard unconstrained optimiser (e.g., Nelder-Mead or L-BFGS-B) might blindly test for negative values, causing the total variance to become negative and crash the pricing engine. By incorporating |a| and |b|, a differentiable barrier is created where the optimiser can search anywhere from $-\infty$ to $\infty$ freely while mathematically guaranteeing a positive total variance.

Finally, this setup also works well with the tail behaviour suggested by the moment-based constraints model. In other words, deep OTM options encode the tails of the risk-neutral distribution; their prices must remain consistent with that distribution. The prior is not just smoothing the data, but it also encodes a theoretically allowed tail growth rate for the implied variance, based on which moments of the underlying asset are finite. The far OTM smile should match the asymptotic limits set by Roger Lee’s moment formula bounds \citep{lee2004moment}. Even though there is little real-option data far out of the money, this structured prior keeps the extrapolated total variance within economic bounds and follows the theoretical maximum growth rates of the volatility smile at extreme strikes.

\section{GP Regression Framework}
We can formally define the Gaussian Process (GP) as a collection of random variables, any of which possesses a joint Gaussian distribution. This way, it provides a non-parametric Bayesian framework for inferring other functions. A GP is specified by its mean function $m(\mathbf{x})$ and its covariance function, or kernel, $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')$. We denote the Gaussian Process prior as:

\[f(\mathbf{x}) \sim \mathcal{GP}(m(\mathbf{x}), k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}'))\]

The mean function, defined as $m(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{x})]$, gives the GP the global topology of the function space. More casually, it represents the prior belief or an assumption about the distribution of an unknown function. It is set to dictate the overall smoothness, behaviour and interpolation of the unknown function. 
In standard machine learning literature, the mean function $m(\mathbf{x})$ is often set to zero for algebraic convenience; however, a financial surface is inherently structurally asymmetrical. This asymmetry can be observed in features such as the persistent equity skew. Hence, the mean function needs careful tuning in order to safely extrapolate quotes in the sparsely populated deep out-of-the-money tails. To address this, the framework explicitly defines $m(\mathbf{x})$ using the parametric prior formulated in Equation \ref{parametric_prior}:

\[m(\mathbf{x}) = T(|a| + |b|k^2 + c k)\]

By substituting this SVI-inspired formulation into the GP mean function, the global structural constraints and theoretical asymptotic tail bounds are embedded directly in the prior distribution. Consequently, the learning burden shifts: the GP is no longer required to infer the macroeconomic shape of the smile from raw data. Instead, it is solely tasked with modelling the residuals. The residuals represent the localised, non-parametric deviations of the observed market data from the parametric mean. These residuals are governed by the covariance kernel, $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \mathbb{E}[(f(\mathbf{x}) - m(\mathbf{x}))(f(\mathbf{x}') - m(\mathbf{x}'))]$. Essentially, this is the covariance between the process values at $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{x'}$, and $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x'})$ measures how these residuals vary together. The kernel describes how points on the volatility surface are related. It controls how smooth the surface is and how quickly the effect of one data point fades as we move across strike and term.

To formalise the regression model, assume a dataset of $n$ noisy total variance observations $\mathbf{y} = \{y_1, \dots, y_n\}^{\top}$ evaluated at input locations $X = \{\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_n\}$. The observation model is given by:

\[y_i = f(\mathbf{x}_i) + \epsilon_i\]

where $\epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_n^2)$ represents independent, identically distributed Gaussian noise. In finance, the noise variance term ($\sigma_n^2$) is essential for accounting for market microstructure noise, discrete tick sizes, and the effects of the bid-ask spread.

Given a set of test inputs $X_*$, the joint distribution of the training targets $\mathbf{y}$ and the predictive function values $\mathbf{f}_*$ under the GP prior is multivariate Gaussian:

\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{y} \\ \mathbf{f}_* \end{bmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N} \left( \begin{bmatrix} m(X) \\ m(X_*) \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} K(X, X) + \sigma_n^2 I & K(X, X_*) \\ K(X_*, X) & K(X_*, X_*) \end{bmatrix} \right)
\label{joint_gaussian}
\end{equation}

where $K(X, X)$ is the $n \times n$ Gram matrix evaluated on the training inputs, $K(X, X_*)$ is the $n \times n_*$ cross-covariance matrix between the training $X$ and test $X_*$ inputs, and $I$ is the identity matrix. The statement \ref{joint_gaussian} asserts that by combining the observed training inputs and unknown test inputs together, the resulting joint distribution is Gaussian under the GP model. Importantly, the left block is the covariance between observed training inputs, plus the noise $\sigma^2I$ term, which indicates that the observed training targets $\mathbf{y}$ are noisy versions of the underlying function values. In this case, the model allows for noise rather than treating the observed data as exact realisations of the latent function. 

By conditioning this joint prior on the observed market data $\mathbf{y}$, we derive the predictive posterior distribution $\mathbf{f}_* | X, \mathbf{y}, X_* \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_*, \Sigma_*)$ as shown in the \ref{sec:schur_complement} derivation. (\TODO{Is this wording fine for pointing the reader to a formal proof?})Equivalently, the above expression states that once we know the training outputs, we can update the joint Gaussian to get a new Gaussian distribution for the unknown test outputs. This is a standard conditioning step in GP regression. The exact matrix equations for the predictive mean and predictive covariance are:

\[\boldsymbol{\mu}_* = m(X_*) + K(X_*, X)[K(X, X) + \sigma_n^2 I]^{-1}(\mathbf{y} - m(X))\]

The predictive mean equation states that the forecast at the new points is the sum of the prior mean and a correction term, which is the covariance between the test and training inputs, weighted by the inverse of the covariance between training inputs, multiplied by the GP deviations from the mean function.

\[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_* = K(X_*, X_*) - K(X_*, X)[K(X, X) + \sigma_n^2 I]^{-1}K(X, X_*)\]

The predictive covariance formula provides the model with the uncertainty after observing the data, where $K(X_*, X_*)$ is the prior uncertainty at the test points minus the part explained by the training data. In practical terms, uncertainty is minimised in dense regions and remains higher where the model must extrapolate.

\section{The Mat\'ern 5/2 Kernel}
While the mean function determines the overall shape of the volatility surface, the covariance kernel governs the local shape at discrete points and the interpolative behaviour of the residuals. The choice of kernel cannot be arbitrary; in financial applications, a covariance function should satisfy strict structural requirements dictated by arbitrage pricing theory. In this work, we adopt an anisotropic Matérn 5/2 kernel; other common kernels (RBF, Matérn 3/2, Rational Quadratic) are viable in general-purpose modelling but have features that are discussed briefly below, which make them less suitable for the present arbitrage-constrained framework. \TODO{Should I add a brief explanation as to why other kernels are not suitable?}

The covariance kernel is a function of two inputs, $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{x}'$. We define two distinct input vectors: $\mathbf{x} = (k, T)^{\top}$ and $\mathbf{x}' = (k', T')^{\top}$. Furthermore, to account for the fundamentally different dynamics along the strike and term-structure dimensions, we define the anisotropic Mahalanobis distance $r$ using independent length-scales $\ell_k$ and $\ell_T$:

$$r(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \sqrt{\frac{(k - k')^2}{\ell_k^2} + \frac{(T - T')^2}{\ell_T^2}}$$

The Matérn $5/2$ covariance function is then formally defined as:

$$k_{5/2}(r) = \sigma_f^2 \left( 1 + \sqrt{5}r + \frac{5}{3}r^2 \right) \exp(-\sqrt{5}r)$$

where $\sigma_f^2$ represents the signal variance, controlling the vertical amplitude of the latent Gaussian Process.
\paragraph{Justification and Differentiability Constraints}

The Matérn class of covariance functions is characterised by its versatility and smoothness parameter $\nu$, which allows for explicit control of the underlying random field. This is especially useful for Implied Volatility surfaces, as they require a twice-differentiable kernel to extract arbitrage-free risk-neutral probabilities from the surface. As established in standard Gaussian Process literature (\citet{rasmussen2006gp}), the sample paths of a GP with a Matérn kernel are $m$ times mean-square differentiable if and only if $\nu > m$. Consequently, setting $\nu = 5/2$ guarantees that the resulting latent function space is exactly twice mean-square differentiable ($\mathcal{C}^2$). The continuous calculus required to prove the kernel supports static arbitrage bounds is provided in Appendix \ref{sec:derivatives_matern_kernel)}. 

This exact $\mathcal{C}^2$ regularity is almost a requirement when modelling implied volatility surfaces. The popular Squared Exponential (or Gaussian) kernel, which is the case when $\nu$ approaches infinity, gives sample paths that are infinitely smooth and analytic. However, this level of smoothness does not match real financial markets, since it over-smooths local volatility spikes and misses the small-scale frictions seen in options trading. The surface becomes silky-smooth, which is great for extrapolating for far OTM options, but pricing in between quotes is too restrictive. On the other hand, if $\nu = 3/2$ or $\nu = 1/2$, the resulting functions are only once differentiable or not differentiable at all, as in the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process.

As mentioned before, the reason for exactly twice-differentiable sample paths is linked to the extraction of the market-implied risk-neutral density. As derived from the Breeden-Litzenberger theorem (\citet{breeden1978prices}), the risk-neutral probability density $p(K,T)$ is strictly proportional to the second partial derivative of the call price with respect to the strike:

$$p(K,T) = e^{rT} \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}$$

The total variance surface $w(k,T)$, mapped by the GP, needs to be inverted using the Black-Scholes formula to calculate call prices. The Black-Scholes model needs a volatility input to produce call prices. Once we know $w(k,T)$, we convert it to IV and price the option. For this to be an arbitration-free, the underlying interpolant must allow stable second-order differentiation. On the other hand, if the sample paths are not twice differentiable, the finite-difference methods used for computing $\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}$ can become unstable, undefined or show singularities, which could falsely indicate butterfly arbitrage violations.

In brief, the Matérn kernel with $\nu = 5/2$ specification offers an ideal balance between smoothness and restriction. It creates smooth curves while still giving the algorithm enough flexibility to accommodate localised peaks and valleys. Additionally, because $\nu$ is a fraction, it involves simpler polynomial and exponential terms that reduce cost complexity and prevent mathematical instability.

\section{The Standard Approach: Point Estimation}
\label{sec:point_estimation_std_approach}
The standard paradigm for training a Gaussian Process in applied machine learning is to select the hyperparameters $\theta$ that maximise the probability of the observed data. This is achieved by maximising the log-marginal likelihood (LML). Conditioning the joint Gaussian prior on the observed target vector $\mathbf{y}$, we define the log-marginal likelihood as:

\begin{equation}
\log p(\mathbf{y} | X, \theta) = -\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}^\top K_y^{-1}\mathbf{y} - \frac{1}{2}\log|K_y| - \frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi)
\label{lml_conditioning}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
K_y = K_\theta + \sigma_n^2 I
\label{noise_covariance_matrix}
\end{equation}

represents the noisy covariance matrix. 


To understand why hyperparameter optimisation often fails in the financial setting, we need to deconstruct the LML function. The kernel hyperparameters are estimated by maximising the Logarithm of Marginal Likelihood (LML), which balances data fit and model complexity. In practical terms, the LML balances model flexibility against data interpolation. This can be seen by decomposing the formula into three distinct theoretical components:

$$\log p(\mathbf{y} | X, \theta) = \underbrace{-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}^\top K_y^{-1}\mathbf{y}}_{\text{Data Fit}} - \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\log|K_y|}_{\text{Complexity Penalty}} - \underbrace{\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi)}_{\text{Normalization Constant}}$$

The fundamental geometry of LML is a balance between the first two terms:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{The Data Fit (Mahalanobis Distance):} The term $-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}^\top K_y^{-1}\mathbf{y}$ measures how well do the observed outputs align with the covariance structure, calculated via the Mahalanobis distance between the observed data and the GP prior mean (which is zeroed out here for the residuals). Additionally, $K_y^{-1}$ reweights the data based on what points should co-vary, not just when the residuals are small but also when those residuals fit the prior's structure. This term strongly prefers highly complex, flexible models. If we allow the Matérn length-scales ($\ell_K$ and $\ell_T$) to shrink toward zero, the covariance between distinct points vanishes, $K_y$ approaches a scaled identity matrix, and the GP essentially memorises the training data. Consequently, the model loses all of its extrapolation capabilities. A model that perfectly interpolates every noisy observation will drive this data fit penalty toward zero, pushing the LML higher.

	\item \textbf{The Complexity Penalty (Log-Determinant):} The term $-\frac{1}{2}\log|K_y|$ acts as an inherent regularisation mechanism. When the length-scales are small, the points one unit apart become weakly correlated. Hence, the kernel matrix is nearly diagonal, changing the log-determinant term. In other words, when the model allows the function to vary widely from point to point, the penalty becomes more negative, reducing the marginal likelihood. This regularisation mechanism serves as the formal RKHS norm constraint preventing jagged surfaces, Appendix \ref{sec:m_theorem_b6} formally defines the norm using functional analysis.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The Origin of Weak Identifiability (Flat Ridges):}
In an ideal scenario, the competition between the Data Fit and the Complexity penalty results in a convex, unimodal peak in the LML landscape. In this case, the algorithm can easily identify a single optimal parameter set $\hat{\theta}$. On the other hand, financial data exhibit characteristic noise and friction (e.g., bid-ask spread noise). Where the LML may exhibit weak identifiability when different hyperparameter combinations yield nearly the same covariance matrix $K_\theta$. This is because the option market data can be successfully interpolated using multiple combinations of strike and maturity length-scales. Mathematically, at a local optimum $\hat{\theta}$, the Hessian of the negative LML measures local curvature. If the Hessian has a very small eigenvalue, then the LML changes slowly in the corresponding direction. Geometrically, this produces a plateau or flat ridges with weakly identifiable hyperparameters.

\paragraph{Standard Software Implementation and the Mathematical Failure of Quasi-Newton Optimisation:} Standard Gaussian Process libraries (such as scikit-learn) navigate this objective landscape using L-BFGS-B (Limited-memory Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno with Bounds), a deterministic Quasi-Newton optimisation algorithm. 

Let $(\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \log p(\mathbf{y} | X, \theta)$. L-BFGS-B seeks to maximise the LML by iteratively solving a local quadratic approximation centred at the current hyperparameter estimate $\theta_k$:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta_k + p) \approx \mathcal{L}(\theta_k) + \nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta_k)^\top p + \frac{1}{2} p^\top \mathcal{H}_k p,$$

where $\mathcal{H}_k = \nabla^2 \mathcal{L}(\theta_k)$ is the Hessian. The Taylor expansion is used because the LML is a complicated nonlinear function of $\theta$ with no closed-form optimal step, but locally around $\theta_k$, any smooth function looks quadratic, as Taylor's theorem guarantees.

However, $\mathcal{H}_k$ is computationally prohibitive to compute exactly. This is due to the extensive differentiation sequences of the kernel matrix, which involve terms of the form $\operatorname{tr}!\left(K_y^{-1} \frac{\partial K_y}{\partial \theta_i}\right)$. These are expensive when $N$ is large. Moreover, the Hessian can become nearly singular, for instance, when hyperparameters are weakly identifiable or approach a boundary, making direct inversion numerically unstable. L-BFGS-B addresses both issues by building a low-rank approximation $B_k^{-1} \approx \mathcal{H}_k^{-1}$ from the last $m$ gradient difference pairs $(s_j, r_j)$ and determining the search direction as $p_k = -B_k^{-1} \nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta_k)$.

The Quasi-Newton approach is computationally efficient in strictly convex regimes, but it can fail in the presence of weak invertibility. To prove this, we must decompose the curvature matrix ($\mathcal{H}$) into its principal directions and magnitudes. We rewrite the Hessian as a sum of eigen-directions, each with an eigenvalue attached. The Hessian $\mathcal{H}$ at a point on a plateau admits an eigenpair ($\lambda_{ridge},v_{ridge}$) with $|\lambda_{ridge}| \approx 0$, where $v_{ridge}$ goes along the tangent direction of the simple curvature, causing the condition number to diverge:

$$\kappa(\mathcal{H}) = \frac{|\lambda_{max}|}{|\lambda_{min}|} \to \infty$$

The divergence breaks the algorithm's mechanics in two ways. 

First, in the search direction update $p_k = -\mathcal{H}^{-1} \nabla \mathcal{L}$ (the complete analytical derivation of this LML gradient is provided in Appendix \ref{sec:lml_gradients}), the inverse eigenvalue $1/\lambda_{min} \to \infty$ artificially amplifies infinitesimal numerical noise in the gradient along the ridge direction. The optimiser overreacts to very small numerical errors in the direction of the plateau. To satisfy the Wolfe conditions and prevent the step size from exploding, the line search algorithm compensates by heavily truncating the step length $\alpha_k$, even though the actual signal is basically flat. The algorithm ends up making tiny moves or getting stuck and thinks it has converged.

Second, the standard L-BFGS-B termination uses a stopping rule; the criterion triggers when the maximum component of the projected gradient falls below a predefined tolerance $\epsilon$:$$\max_i |(\text{proj} \nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta_k))_i| < \epsilon$$ On a flat ridge, the algorithm thinks it has converged because the gradient is below that threshold. In reality, the surface is nearly flat along that direction. As the directional derivative vanishes ($\nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta) \cdot v_{ridge} \approx 0$), the optimiser mistakenly interprets this flat gradient as evidence of a true stationary point. It prematurely terminates the line search, declaring convergence at a suboptimal coordinate $\hat{\theta}_{local}$ rather than traversing the ridge and actually looking for a better one. As will be empirically demonstrated in Chapter 4, this structural pathology causes point-estimated models to become highly sensitive to random initialisations, trapping them in local optima and yielding unpredictable, financially unstable volatility surfaces.

\section{Restart Instability}
\label{sec:restart_instability}
The ultimate choice of hyperparameters is heavily dependent on the initial position of the optimiser. The issue of the optimiser's sensitivity to initialisation coordinates is made especially clear when navigating a flat ridge, as in Figure \ref{fig:topology-2026-06-12}. The standard practice in Gaussian Process regression is to run the L-BFGS-B algorithm from multiple random starting points (restarts) and select the hyperparameter configuration that yields the highest LML. 

During the empirical evaluation for 2026-06-11, the pipeline made 20 restarts. The process repeatedly triggered the \texttt{ConvergenceWarning} flag, explicitly indicating that the optimal value was outside the specified lower length-scale bounds. Importantly, the restarts did not converge to a single consensus estimate. Additionally, the standard deviation of the final LML values across the restarts was high at 90.02.

To understand why the initialisation coordinate carries such importance for the resulting estimate, we must mathematically break down the mechanics of the L-BFGS-B algorithm in the presence of a non-convex plateau. Let $\theta_0^{(1)}$ and $\theta_0^{(2)}$ be two distinct random initialisation vectors. The optimiser defines a descent direction $p_k$ and relies on the strong Wolfe conditions to dictate the step length $\alpha_k$:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta_k + \alpha_k p_k) &\leq \mathcal{L}(\theta_k) + c_1 \alpha_k \nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta_k)^\top p_k \quad \text{(Sufficient Decrease)} \\
    |\nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta_k + \alpha_k p_k)^\top p_k| &\leq c_2 |\nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta_k)^\top p_k| \quad \text{(Curvature Condition)} \label{eq:curvature_condition}
\end{align}
where $0 < c_1 < c_2 < 1$. When the initialisation point $\theta_0^{(1)}$ lands on one side of the plateau, the algorithm rapidly descends the steep walls of the objective function, successfully satisfying the Wolfe conditions. However, the moment the trajectory reaches the flat ridge, the gradient magnitude collapses ($\|\nabla \mathcal{L}\| \to 0$) and the algorithm declares convergence. 

At this point, the curvature condition (Equation \ref{eq:curvature_condition}) is the final stage in which the decision is made about whether to keep the step size $\alpha_k$. When the algorithm descends into the valley, any step size yields a negligible change in the direction derivative $\nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta_k)$. In other words, neither the sufficient decrease nor the curvature conditions will be satisfied, leading the algorithm to converge falsely. Consequently, the final estimate $\hat{\theta}^{(1)}$ is entirely dependent on the specific location where the trajectory of $\theta_0^{(1)}$ first intercepted the ridge. Similarly, a different initialisation coordinate $\theta_0^{(2)}$ will intercept the plateau at a different location and terminate there. The resulting estimate of $\theta_0^{(n)}$ will yield different parameters; none of them will be the optimal true maximum likelihood estimate, but rather just a random artefact of the optimiser's starting position.

\section{From Optimisation to Integration}
In the previous chapter, we demonstrated that the topology of the log-marginal space for the financial volatility surfaces is quite ill-posed and features weak hyperparameter identifiability. This is because deterministic optimisation algorithms (e.g., L-BFGS-B) seek to maximise this objective function without accounting for the fact that the hyperparameter space cannot be collapsed to a single point estimate, which is not a definitive optimum. On the other hand, it is an arbitrary coordinate dictated by the optimiser's initialisation and line-search termination criteria. 

To address this structural fragility, this thesis examines the problem through the lens of integration rather than optimisation. Instead of attempting to find a single optimal estimate, we will adopt a fully Bayesian framework. This seeks to keep the full probability distribution over what those potential parameters could be, given the data. By applying Bayes' theorem to the GP hyperparameters, we define the posterior distribution as:

\begin{equation}
p(\theta \mid \mathbf{y}, X) = \frac{p(\mathbf{y} \mid X, \theta) p(\theta)}{\int p(\mathbf{y} \mid X, \theta^\prime) p(\theta^\prime) d\theta^\prime}
\label{eq:bayesian_theorem}
\end{equation}

Where $p(\mathbf{y} \mid X, \theta)$ is the exact marginal likelihood defined in Chapter 3, and $p(\theta)$ is a joint prior distribution placed over the hyperparameters (such as the Matérn length-scales and noise variance) to constrain them to strictly positive, financially sensible bounds.

The advantage of this shift can be observed through forecasting. The standard point estimation methods rely on the rigid assumption that $\theta^*$ represents the absolute truth for predictions at unseen test inputs. Resulting in the condition predictive distribution $p(\mathbf{f}_* \mid X_*, X, \mathbf{y}, \theta^*)$. In contrast, the fully Bayesian predictive distribution marginalises out the hyperparameter uncertainty entirely by integrating the conditional predictions over the full posterior:

$$p(\mathbf{f}_* \mid X_*, X, \mathbf{y}) = \int p(\mathbf{f}_* \mid X_*, X, \mathbf{y}, \theta) p(\theta \mid \mathbf{y}, X) d\theta$$

In practical terms, this integral represents a continuous ensemble of Gaussian Processes. Rather than just picking one of them, which can lead to unstable extrapolation if the optimiser lands on a bad spot. The Bayesian integral avoids this by hedging across all plausible explanations of the data. More specifically, the integral averages the predictive surfaces over all possible hyperparameter combinations, weighting them by their posterior probabilities. This approach handles cases where the data weakly identify whether a local market movement is driven by a short strike length scale ($\ell_K$) or elevated signal variance ($\sigma_f^2$); the Bayesian predictive surface hedges between these competing scenarios. This structural averaging naturally avoids extreme localised extrapolations. Consequently, lowering the out-of-sample pricing error.

It is important to note that computing the exact Bayesian predictive distribution is practically impossible for a Gaussian Process regression. The primary reason for that is the denominator in the \eqref{eq:bayesian_theorem}, which lacks a closed-form analytical solution due to the highly nonlinear relationship between the hyperparameters and the inverted Matérn covariance matrix. As a consequence, this integral cannot be computed directly. Instead, we must approximate it using stochastic numerical methods. Hence, justifying the transition of analytical calculus to Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulation.

\section{Markov Chain Monte Carlo}
Having established that the computation of the Bayesian predictive integral exactly is analytically infeasible, we take the approach of Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods to construct an approximation of the posterior. The objective is to generate a range of dependent samples $\{\theta^{(1)}, \theta^{(2)}, \dots, \theta^{(S)}\}$ such that, as $S \to \infty$, the empirical distribution converges to the exact target prior $p(\theta \mid \mathbf{y}, X)$.

The standard approach to this approximation problem is the Random Walk Metropolis (RWM) algorithm. Which takes the current parameter value and at each iteration step $s$, the algorithm suggests a nearby candidate $\theta^\prime$ by adding isotropic Gaussian noise:

$$\theta^\prime \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta^{(s)}, \Sigma_{prop})$$


where $\Sigma_{prop} = \sigma^2 I$ is the isotropic variance and controls the step size. The proposal is then accepted or rejected based on the Metropolis acceptance probability $\alpha$: (\TODO{should I explain how the Metropolis sampling works?})

$$\alpha = \min\left(1, \frac{p(\theta^\prime \mid \mathbf{y}, X)}{p(\theta^{(s)} \mid \mathbf{y}, X)}\right)$$

The RWM algorithm is guaranteed to asymptotically converge to the true posterior in the limit of infinite iterations, but becomes very inefficient in $d$-dimensional space. Its mixing time increases substantially because, by the \textbf{concentration of measure} principle, most of the posterior mass lies in a thin \textbf{typical set} rather than near the mode. Hence, isotropic proposals either have low acceptance rates or slow convergence.

More specifically, the RWM relies on a gradient-free proposal distribution, in turn leading to a structural trade-off between the step size $\sigma$ and the acceptance rate $\alpha$. If the step size $\sigma$ is chosen to be small, the candidate state remains within the typical set ($\alpha \approx 1$), and the exploration degrades into a diffusive random walk. After $N$ steps, the distance scales like $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N})$, so the algorithm takes a long time to explore the full ridge geometry.

Conversely, if the steps taken by the algorithm are large. The isotropic candidate state will often be outside the narrow bounds of the typical set. Where the target density $p(\theta^\prime \mid \mathbf{y}, X) \to 0$. Consequently, the acceptance probability $\alpha$ plummets, thereby paralysing the chain. Mathematically, for the acceptance probability ratio $\alpha$ to be nonzero, the size step must be scaled down by a factor of $\mathcal{O}(d^{-1/2})$ at the cost of stagnating the exploration of the surface in higher dimensions.

To avoid this diffusive, random-walk behaviour, we replace the isotropic, gradient-free candidate state model with a gradient-informed MCMC algorithm based on $\nabla_\theta \log p(\theta \mid \mathbf{y}, X)$. Which will actively steer the Markov chain along the contours of the typical set.

\section{Hamiltonian Monte Carlo}
A practical solution to the previous problem is the Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC), which reformulates the statistical sampling problem as a deterministic simulation of classical mechanics. The algorithm is able to explore the high-dimensional typical set by introducing an auxiliary momentum variable and using information from the gradient of the target density to construct a vector field. 

\subsection{Phase Space and the Hamiltonian}

Let the Gaussian Process hyperparameter vector $\theta$ map to a position vector $q \in \mathbb{R}^d$. To model this as a physical system, we introduce a fictitious, independent momentum vector $p \in \mathbb{R}^d$ representing an additional random push. The original parameter space is thus expanded into a $2d$-dimensional \textbf{phase space} $(q, p)$. This approach allows us to treat the hyperparameters as a point moving in the mathematical space. Essentially, we are artificially expanding the space so the sampling method can behave more like a physical system. Intuitively, we are applying a concept that serves as a computational helper, because we do not want the momentum variable to alter the target posterior over the original parameters. We explicitly define the joint distribution of the target variables and the auxiliary momentum as statistically independent: $p(q, p) = p(q)p(p)$. Where the Hamiltonian $H(q, p)$ is the energy of this artificial system. In statistical mechanics, the canonical distribution relates the joint density to the energy function $H(q, p)$:

$$p(q, p) = \frac{1}{Z} \exp(-H(q, p))$$

where $Z$ is the partition function. Hence, the joint distribution can be defined up to a constant. Because we defined the position and momentum to be independent, the Hamiltonian can be separated into Potential Energy $U(q)$ and Kinetic Energy $K(p)$:

$$H(q, p) = U(q) + K(p)$$

The potential energy $U(q)$ is defined as the negative logarithm of the unnormalized target posterior density under the Gaussian Process framework:

$$U(q) = -\log p(\mathbf{y} \mid X, q) - \log p(q)$$

The first term corresponds to the negative log-marginal likelihood of the Matérn $5/2$ covariance structure that we defined in Chapter 3. The second term, $-\log p(q)$, accounts for the prior of the hyperparameters. Furthermore, the strictly positive Matérn parameters $\theta = \{\ell_K, \ell_T, \sigma_f, \sigma_n\}$ are mapped to the real line $q = \log(\theta)$ to ensure that the HMC particle traverses a smooth plane and doesn't diverge into numerical singularities during integration.

\subsection{Hamiltonian Dynamics}
The evolution of the system through phase space follows the rules of motion given by Hamilton's equations. They constitute a system of $2d$ coupled first-order ordinary differential equations that tell us how the state evolves over time.


$$\frac{dq_i}{dt} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} = [M^{-1} p]_i$$

$$\frac{dp_i}{dt} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i} = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial q_i}$$

In practical terms, the first equation says that the position changes at a rate determined by the momentum. Hence, if momentum is larger in some direction, the parameter moves faster in that direction. While the other equation describes how the momentum changes according to the negative slope of the potential energy landscape. So, as the negative log-posterior increases, the particle drifts in some direction; the negative gradient pushes the particle back towards more probable regions, allowing it to rapidly traverse the flat ridges rather than wandering blindly through phase space.

\subsection{Theoretical Guarantees: Conservation, Volume, and Reversibility}

In order for the Hamiltonian dynamics to be valid as a mechanism for a Markov chain, the continuous-time evolution must satisfy three strict conditions: energy conservation, volume preservation, and time reversibility. In other words, for the HMC to efficiently explore the surface's geometry, it must obey the three laws so it doesn't change the posterior distribution it samples from.

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Energy Conservation}
The energy conservation rule keeps the Hamiltonian invariant, conserving energy over time. 

\textit{Proof.} By applying the multivariate chain rule and substituting Hamilton's equations, the time derivative becomes $H$:

\begin{equation}
\frac{dH}{dt} = \sum_{i=1}^d \left( \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i} \frac{dq_i}{dt} + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} \frac{dp_i}{dt} \right)
\label{energy_conservation}
\end{equation}

$$\frac{dH}{dt} = \sum_{i=1}^d \left( \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i} \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i} \right) = 0$$


Because $\frac{dH}{dt} = 0$, the particle glides along isocontours of the joint density $p(q, p)$. Hence, the trajectory does not move toward higher or lower Hamiltonian values and remains at the same probability level rather than exploring them. In practice, we cannot integrate Hamilton's equations analytically, so we approximate the continuous dynamics using the Leapfrog method reviewed in Chapter 5.4.

	\item \textbf{Volume Preservation (Liouville's Theorem)}
Another fundamental condition is that Hamiltonian dynamics must preserve volume in phase space. Mathematically, this implies that a region of phase space mapped forward in time will retain its original volume. 

\textit{Proof.} Consider the vector field $\mathbf{V}$ governing the flow in phase space, defined as $\mathbf{V} = \left( \frac{dq}{dt}, \frac{dp}{dt} \right)$. The divergence of this vector field is:


\begin{equation}
    \nabla \cdot \mathbf{V} = \sum_{i=1}^d \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial q_i} \frac{dq_i}{dt} + \frac{\partial}{\partial p_i} \frac{dp_i}{dt} \right)
\end{equation}


Substituting Hamilton's equations yields:


\begin{align*}
    \nabla \cdot \mathbf{V} &= \sum_{i=1}^d \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial q_i} \left( \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} \right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial p_i} \left( -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i} \right) \right) \\
    &= \sum_{i=1}^d \left( \frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial q_i \partial p_i} - \frac{\partial^2 H}{\partial p_i \partial q_i} \right) = 0
\end{align*}


By Clairaut's theorem, the second partial derivatives of $H$ are equal if $H$ is smooth and continuous. Thereby, the two terms cancel, and the divergence becomes zero. This symplectic property is vital because it implies that the determinant of the Jacobian of the Hamiltonian flow is strictly equal to 1, completely bypassing the need to compute complex Jacobian determinants during the Metropolis acceptance step.

	\item \textbf{Time Reversibility} 
The mapping is invariant under time reversal. If the system is simulated forward for time $t$, negating the momentum and simulating forward for time $t$ returns the system to its exact initial state. This symmetry is mandatory for ensuring detailed balance in the resulting Markov chain.

\textit{Proof.} Let the state of the system be defined by the coordinates $(q, p)$ evolving under continuous time $t$. Consider the time-reversal transformation defined by mapping time $t \mapsto \tau = -t$ and simultaneously negating the auxiliary momentum $p \mapsto \tilde{p} = -p$, while position remains unchanged $q \mapsto \tilde{q} = q$. 

Because the kinetic energy $K(p) = \frac{1}{2} p^\top M^{-1} p$ is a quadratic form, the Hamiltonian $H(q, p) = U(q) + K(p)$ is strictly symmetric with respect to momentum, ensuring that $H(q, p) = H(\tilde{q}, \tilde{p})$. 

Evaluating Hamilton's equations of motion under this transformation via the chain rule yields the position derivative:
$$ \frac{d\tilde{q}}{d\tau} = \frac{dq}{d(-t)} = -\frac{dq}{dt} = -\frac{\partial H(q, p)}{\partial p} $$

Because the partial derivative of an even quadratic function is an odd function, evaluating $\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}$ at $-p$ yields $-\frac{\partial H}{\partial \tilde{p}}$. Substituting this into the previous equation results in:
$$ \frac{d\tilde{q}}{d\tau} = -\left( -\frac{\partial H(\tilde{q}, \tilde{p})}{\partial \tilde{p}} \right) = \frac{\partial H(\tilde{q}, \tilde{p})}{\partial \tilde{p}} $$

Similarly, evaluating the evolution of the transformed momentum yields:
$$ \frac{d\tilde{p}}{d\tau} = \frac{d(-p)}{d(-t)} = \frac{dp}{dt} = -\frac{\partial H(q, p)}{\partial q} $$

Because the potential energy $U(q)$ is independent of momentum, the spatial gradient remains completely unaffected by the sign of $p$:
$$ \frac{d\tilde{p}}{d\tau} = -\frac{\partial H(\tilde{q}, \tilde{p})}{\partial \tilde{q}} $$

The transformed phase-space variables $(\tilde{q}, \tilde{p})$ perfectly satisfy the exact original Hamiltonian equations of motion with respect to reversed time $\tau$. Therefore, the continuous-time Hamiltonian dynamics are strictly reversible, allowing the Metropolis-Hastings acceptance step to maintain detailed balance. \hfill $\blacksquare$

\end{enumerate}
\subsection{The No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS)}
\label{subsec:nuts}

While standard Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) eliminates the diffusive random-walk behavior of traditional Metropolis-Hastings algorithms, its practical efficiency depends critically on two user-defined parameters: the discrete integration step size $\epsilon$ and the trajectory length $L$ (or total integration time $T = L\epsilon$). 

In complex topological landscapes—such as the flat, weakly identifiable Log-Marginal Likelihood (LML) ridges of Gaussian Process volatility models—manually tuning $L$ is problematic:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Suboptimal $L$ ($L$ too small):} The particle takes insufficient steps across phase space, causing the sampler to degrade into a high-variance, diffusive random walk.
    \item \textbf{Excessive $L$ ($L$ too large):} The particle follows a long Hamiltonian trajectory that eventually loops back onto itself (takes a "U-turn"), wasting computationally expensive $\mathcal{O}(N^3)$ Cholesky gradient evaluations without expanding the exploration of the posterior target density.
\end{itemize}

To resolve this tuning dilemma, Hoffman and Gelman (2014) introduced the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS), an extension of HMC that dynamically determines the optimal trajectory length $L$ at every iteration while strictly preserving time-reversibility and detailed balance.

\subsubsection{Symplectic Integration and Dynamic Trajectories}

At each iteration of the Markov chain, NUTS constructs a Hamiltonian trajectory starting from the current state $(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{p})$ using the discrete-time, area-preserving \emph{Leapfrog integrator}. Given a step size $\epsilon$ and mass matrix $\mathbf{M}$, a single leapfrog step updates position and auxiliary momentum as follows:
\begin{align}
    \mathbf{p}\left(t + \frac{\epsilon}{2}\right) &= \mathbf{p}(t) - \frac{\epsilon}{2} \nabla_{\mathbf{q}} U(\mathbf{q}(t)), \label{eq:leapfrog1} \\
    \mathbf{q}(t + \epsilon) &= \mathbf{q}(t) + \epsilon \mathbf{M}^{-1} \mathbf{p}\left(t + \frac{\epsilon}{2}\right), \label{eq:leapfrog2} \\
    \mathbf{p}(t + \epsilon) &= \mathbf{p}\left(t + \frac{\epsilon}{2}\right) - \frac{\epsilon}{2} \nabla_{\mathbf{q}} U(\mathbf{q}(t + \epsilon)). \label{eq:leapfrog3}
\end{align}

Rather than integrating forward for a fixed number of steps $L$, NUTS generates trajectories by building a recursive binary tree. At tree height $j \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$, the algorithm selects a direction $v \in \{-1, +1\}$ uniformly at random and integrates $2^j$ leapfrog steps either backward ($v = -1$) or forward ($v = +1$) in time. This recursive doubling generates a trajectory spanning $2^{j+1}-1$ candidate states, maintaining explicit reversibility.

\subsubsection{The No-U-Turn Stopping Criterion}

To determine when the trajectory begins to double back on itself, NUTS evaluates a geometric termination criterion. Let $\mathbf{q}_{\text{left}}$ and $\mathbf{q}_{\text{right}}$ denote the leftmost and rightmost position vectors of the candidate states within the current subtree, and let $\mathbf{p}_{\text{left}}$ and $\mathbf{p}_{\text{right}}$ be their respective momentum vectors. 

The trajectory stops growing when the derivative of the squared Euclidean distance between $\mathbf{q}_{\text{left}}$ and $\mathbf{q}_{\text{right}}$ becomes negative. Mathematically, the expansion is halted as soon as:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{dt} \frac{1}{2} \|\mathbf{q}_{\text{right}} - \mathbf{q}_{\text{left}}\|^2 < 0.
\end{equation}
By applying the chain rule, this condition is evaluated at both endpoints of the tree trajectory:
\begin{equation}
    (\mathbf{q}_{\text{right}} - \mathbf{q}_{\text{left}})^\top \mathbf{p}_{\text{right}} < 0 \quad \text{or} \quad (\mathbf{q}_{\text{right}} - \mathbf{q}_{\text{left}})^\top \mathbf{p}_{\text{left}} < 0.
    \label{eq:u_turn_condition}
\end{equation}

When the condition in Equation~\ref{eq:u_turn_condition} is satisfied, continuing integration would pull the particle back toward its starting location. Tree expansion terminates immediately, and a new parameter state $\mathbf{q}^{(s+1)}$ is sampled from the candidate trajectory using a multinomial scheme weighted by the joint posterior density $\exp(-H(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{p}))$, ensuring unbiased sampling from the exact target distribution.

\subsubsection{Warmup Adaptation: Dual Averaging and Inverse Mass Matrix}

To eliminate the need for manual tuning of the remaining hyperparameters—the integration step size $\epsilon$ and the diagonal mass matrix $\mathbf{M}$—NUTS utilizes a dual-phase adaptation protocol during the warmup stage (\texttt{num\_warmup = 500}).

\paragraph{1. Step Size Adaptation via Dual Averaging:} 
During warmup, NUTS adjusts the step size $\epsilon$ using Nesterov's dual-averaging scheme. The algorithm targets an optimal average acceptance probability $\delta_{\text{target}}$ (defaulted to $0.80$). At iteration $m$, $\epsilon_m$ is updated via:
\begin{equation}
    H_m = \left(1 - \frac{1}{m + t_0}\right) H_{m-1} + \frac{1}{m + t_0} \left(\delta_{\text{target}} - \alpha_m\right),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \log \epsilon_m = \mu - \frac{\sqrt{m}}{\gamma} H_m,
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_m$ is the observed acceptance probability of the $m$-th trajectory, $\mu = \log(10 \epsilon_0)$, and $\gamma, t_0$ are stabilization parameters.

\paragraph{2. Mass Matrix Estimation:}
Simultaneously, the inverse mass matrix $\mathbf{M}^{-1}$ is continuously updated during the warmup phase by computing the sample covariance of the target parameters. In presence of anisotropic length-scales, estimating $\mathbf{M}^{-1}$ rescales the phase-space geometry, transforming elongated elliptical contours into isotropic hyperspheres.

\subsubsection{Computational Implementation in JAX and NumPyro}

In the implementation developed for this thesis, the NUTS engine is executed using the \texttt{NumPyro} library backed by \texttt{JAX}. This architecture affords significant mathematical and computational advantages when sampling over Gaussian Process hyperparameters:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Unconstrained Parameter Mapping:} To prevent numerical integration boundaries, the constrained positive kernel parameters are mapped to unconstrained real space $\mathbf{q} \in \mathbb{R}^4$:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{q} = \begin{bmatrix} 
        \log \ell_k \\ 
        \log \ell_t \\ 
        \log \sigma_f \\ 
        \log \sigma_n 
        \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^4.
    \end{equation}
    The exact Gaussian Process model transforms these unbounded variables back via exponential maps ($\ell_k = \exp(q_1)$, etc.), while evaluating logarithmic Gaussian priors ($\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and $\mathcal{N}(-3, 1)$) to form $U(\mathbf{q})$.
    
    \item \textbf{Automatic Differentiation through Cholesky Decompositions:} Evaluating the gradient $\nabla_{\mathbf{q}} U(\mathbf{q})$ at every leapfrog step requires differentiating through the $N \times N$ dense covariance matrix $\mathbf{K}_y = \mathbf{K}_{5/2} + \sigma_n^2 \mathbf{I}$. \texttt{JAX} compiles the anisotropic Matérn $5/2$ kernel matrix computation and the subsequent Cholesky factorization ($\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^\top = \mathbf{K}_y$) directly into optimized XLA primitives, computing reverse-mode vector-Jacobian products (VJPs) with minimal overhead.
    
    \item \textbf{Parallel Chain Execution:} By invoking \texttt{numpyro.set\_host\_device\_count(2)}, two independent MCMC chains (\texttt{num\_chains = 2}) are sampled in parallel. Each chain undergoes $500$ warmup iterations and $1000$ sampling iterations, yielding $2000$ posterior draws per date. Running multiple chains enables explicit convergence diagnostics via the Gelman-Rubin split-$\hat{R}$ metric ($\hat{R} < 1.05$).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Data Ingestion and Preprocessing}

The empirical analysis utilises European-style option chains on the S\&P 500 Index (SPX). Due to the sheer density of the SPX options market, evaluating the entire continuous surface simultaneously is computationally expensive and mathematically unnecessary for the sake of hyperparameter inference. Therefore, the data ingestion pipeline is designed to construct a representative discrete term structure.

The market data was extracted via the Yahoo Finance API. A number of regimes were selected to test the HMC sampler, ranging from high-distress volatility to sustained-volatility regimes. The pipeline extracts a stratified cross-section of maturities, specifically targeting near-term (2 weeks), medium-term (5 and 12 weeks), and long-term (20 weeks) expirations. This provides the Gaussian Process with enough data to model a vast topological space that captures both steep, convex skew of short-dated options and the flatter, asymptotic behaviour of long-dated contracts. 

For each maturity, the raw option chains are filtered using a strict preprocessing protocol to remove erroneous quotes and microstructure noise that will interfere with the construction of the Log-Marginal Likelihood landscape:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Liquidity and Validity Constraints:} 
    The market exhibits microstructure noise for various reasons, including, but not limited to, stale quotes and widening bid-ask spreads for OTM options. The noise changes the covariance matrix by adding a noise component (equation \ref{noise_covariance_matrix}). Thereby, altering conditioning, effective weighting, and hyperparameter estimation. To prevent instability, we strictly filter the dataset. Any option with a trading volume of zero, or missing bid/ask quotes ($Bid \le 0$ or $Ask \le 0$), is discarded. Furthermore, quotes with extreme implied volatilities ($\sigma_{\text{imp}} \le 0.01$ or $\sigma_{\text{imp}} \ge 2.5$) are removed, as these often are pricing errors or highly illiquid strikes.
    \item \textbf{Coordinate Transformation:} 
    A more natural coordinate system than the regular $(K,T)$ is the $k = \log(K/S_0)$. The log-moneyness represents a relative coordinate space that puts the strike price $K$ into perspective with respect to the spot price $S_0$. To ensure the Matérn kernel operates on a stable, scale-invariant domain, the strikes are mapped to log-moneyness $k = \log(K/S_0)$. Simultaneously, the expiration dates are converted into an annualised time-to-maturity metric $T$.
    \item \textbf{Target Variable Construction:} 
    As mentioned in Section \ref{subsec:static-arbitrage}, the target variable for the Gaussian Process is not the raw implied volatility, but rather the total implied variance. Following Gatheral's SVI framework, the filtered implied volatilities are squared and scaled by maturity: $w(k,T) = \sigma_{\text{imp}}^2(k,T) \, T$. This ensures the calendar arbitrage monotonicity constraints detailed in Section \ref{subsubsec:calendar-arbitrage} can be accurately evaluated.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
To measure the performance of the competing inference methodologies, we must separate the evaluation framework into global predictive accuracy and local financial consistency. As established in Section \ref{subsec:static-arbitrage}, a mathematically smooth interpolant is financially invalid if it permits arbitrage. Therefore, the models are evaluated across the following three numerical metrics:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Out-of-Sample Predictive Accuracy (RMSE):}
	The Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) measures the out-of-sample accuracy to determine the global fit of the model, which is strictly evaluated on the testing set. Let $N_{\text{test}}$ be the number of unseen option quotes, $y_i$ be the observed total implied variance, and $\hat{y}_i$ be the model's predictive mean. The out-of-sample RMSE is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \text{RMSE}_{\text{OOS}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{\text{test}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{test}}} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}
\end{equation}

This metric penalises models that overfit the training data's microstructural noise by measuring their generalisation error on unseen coordinates.

	\item \textbf{Calendar Arbitrage Violations:}
	While Section \ref{subsec:static-arbitrage} defines the calendar arbitrage as a continuous monotonicity condition ($\partial w / \partial T \geq 0$), the numerical implementation is done using forward finite differences. Let $\mathbf{T} = \{T_1, T_2, \dots, T_m\}$ represent the sorted unique maturities. For each strike coordinate, the discrete derivative is computed and formally recorded as calendar arbitrage. Later summed whenever the discrete variance increment is negative:
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{j=1}^{m-1} \mathbb{I} \left( \frac{w(k, T_{j+1}) - w(k, T_j)}{T_{j+1} - T_j} < 0 \right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function.

	\item \textbf{Butterfly Arbitrage Violations:}
	Similarly, the theoretical condition for the absence of butterfly arbitrage requires the risk-neutral probability density to remain non-negative, corresponding to a strictly convex call price curve ($\partial^2 C / \partial K^2 \geq 0$). To calculate it, we invert the predictive surface $w(k, T)$ to obtain European call prices $C(K, T)$ using the standard Black-Scholes pricing formula because the GP interpolates in total variance space. We evaluate the second derivative using a numerical approximation at the midpoint. Given a sorted grid of unique strike prices $\mathbf{K} = \{K_1, K_2, \dots, K_p\}$, let $\Delta C_i = C(K_{i+1}, T) - C(K_i, T)$ and $\Delta K_i = K_{i+1} - K_i$. The evaluation becomes:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} \approx \frac{\frac{\Delta C_{i+1}}{\Delta K_{i+1}} - \frac{\Delta C_i}{\Delta K_i}}{\Delta K_i}
\end{equation}

The pipeline aggregates total butterfly violations by applying this finite-difference array operation and counting every instance in which the discrete second derivative falls below zero across all evaluated maturities.
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{Conclusion}
\section{The Pathology of Point Estimation}
This dissertation set out to resolve the structural instability present in non-parametric volatility surface models, specifically addressing the failure of the deterministic point estimation approach to Gaussian Processes during market turmoil. The core mathematical discovery of this research is that Log-Marginal Likelihood (LML) for options is often non-convex, featuring a weakly identifiable hyperparameter space. This directly undermines the standard assumption that a single, optimal set of hyperparameters can be isolated from the market data.

Most importantly, this topological mapping reveals a structural flaw, not in the deterministic L-BFGS-B optimiser, but within the Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) objective it is instructed to solve. The L-BFGS-B algorithm is a highly efficient quasi-Newton method; it mathematically succeeds in finding the exact coordinates that maximise the probability of the observed training data. However, in the context of chaotic financial markets, the idea of absolute optimum is a mathematical trap. The absolute optimum corresponds to shorter length scales, where the LML score is highest because it aggressively memorises in-sample microstructural friction, such as bid-ask spreads and asynchronous tick sizes. Consequently, this achieves a nominally lower OOS RMSE in certain regimes.

Therefore, by restricting the model to collapse its hyperparameter uncertainty into a single noise-fitting coordinate on a flat ridge, point estimation transforms the Gaussian Process from generalisable, out-of-sample forecasting into a highly brittle, overfitted function. Directly contradicting the fundamental goal of MLE.

\section{The Accuracy-Consistency Trade-off}
The consequence of this overfitting introduces a dangerous paradigm into quantitative trading: the financial illusion of predictive accuracy. A low generalisation error becomes meaningless unless accompanied by strict arbitrage constraints.

The empirical findings of this research highlight the instability of the deterministic point estimation approach under these economic constraints. Across the evaluated historical data, the point estimator achieved a 61.9\% butterfly arbitrage failure rate. Additionally, this instability regularly escalated into systemic dual-violations, failing both calendar and butterfly conditions on 14.3\% of the evaluated dates, with peak failures occurring during the severe market shocks of March 2020 and the summer of 2023. Thereby supporting the claim that the pursuit of the absolute maximum likelihood frequently guaranteed negative risk-neutral probabilities.

Conversely, the Bayesian framework adopts a role of a structural regulariser; the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) integrates across the entire weakly identifiable posterior ridge, generating an ensemble of predictive surfaces. By averaging them, the fatal structural flaw of the point estimator is resolved. Furthermore, by replacing point estimation with Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) inference, the chaotic microstructural noise cancels itself out due to the marginalisation process of the framework.

Ultimately, this regularisation exposes an accuracy-consistency trade-off between out-of-sample error and financial validity. This compromise is especially noticeable during steady or low-volatility regimes such as October 2022 and June 2023. The Bayesian model accepts a small penalty in generalisation error compared to the deterministic optimiser. However, this marginal reduction in accuracy is the mandatory price for an economically admissible surface, which guarantees the eradication of structural arbitrage.

\section{Computational Limitations and Future Work}
While the Bayesian Hamiltonian Monte Carlo framework decisively outperforms the deterministic point estimation approach in preserving economic admissibility, its deployment and upkeep during live trading sessions are heavily constrained by computational complexity. Although the time complexity of both algorithms is $\mathcal{O}(N^3)$, the fundamental mathematical bottleneck is the computationally expensive Cholesky decomposition of the covariance matrix. The paramount difference between them comes from the constant factor, which represents the number of times the algorithms have to execute this expensive $\mathcal{O}(N^3)$ decomposition. Based on the hyperparameter configurations used in this study, evaluating the full posterior via Hamiltonian Monte Carlo requires roughly 1,000 times more computational overhead than deterministic point estimation.

It is reasonable to assert that the current HMC implementation is most optimally suited for end-of-day settlement pricing, risk management, and portfolio margin calculations. In this scenario, the extreme accuracy and strictly arbitrage-free surfaces are important, but execution latency is a secondary concern. Furthermore, the current computational complexity of the NUTS sampler is expensive and impractical for High Frequency Trading (HFT), where volatility surfaces must be updated on sub-millisecond timescales.

The logical progression for future quantitative research is to bridge this computational gap by exploring Variational Inference (VI) or Sparse Gaussian Processes. By framing Bayesian inference as an optimisation problem rather than a stochastic sampling problem, VI has the potential to approximate the true Bayesian posterior in a fraction of the time. Successfully implementing a Variational Gaussian Process could theoretically maintain the powerful regularising benefits of hyperparameter ensembling while achieving the near-deterministic execution speeds required for high-frequency trading floors.

\section{The Schur Complement and the GP Predictive Distribution}
\label{sec:schur_complement}
This derivation provides the formal proof for how a Gaussian Process conditions its predictions based on observed market data. By applying the Schur complement to a partitioned block covariance matrix, we isolate the exact conditional mean and covariance for unseen test coordinates.

\subsection{The Setup: The Joint Multivariate Gaussian}

We begin by defining the joint probability distribution of our observed, noisy market targets (Total Implied Variance, denoted as $y$) and our unobserved, noise-free predictive targets denoted as $f_*$. The joint distribution becomes multivariate Gaussian, assuming the data has been scaled or the parametric prior has been subtracted:

$$\begin{bmatrix} y \\ f_* \end{bmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N} \left( \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0} \\ \mathbf{0} \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} K(X, X) + \alpha I & K(X, X_*) \\ K(X_*, X) & K(X_*, X_*) \end{bmatrix} \right)$$

For algebraic clarity, we will denote the covariance matrix into blocks as follows:

\begin{itemize}
	\item $K_{yy} = K(X, X) + \alpha I$ (The $n \times n$ training covariance with the regularization nugget $\alpha$)
	\item $K_{y*} = K(X, X_*)$ (The $n \times n_*$ cross-covariance between training and test inputs)
	\item $K_{*y} = K(X_*, X)$ (The $n_* \times n$ cross-covariance, which is $K_{y*}^T$)
	\item $K_{} = K(X_*, X_*)$ (The $n_* \times n_*$ prior test covariance)
\end{itemize}

Thus, the joint covariance matrix is $\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} K_{yy} & K_{y*} \\ K_{*y} & K_{} \end{bmatrix}$.

\subsection{The Derivation: Affine Transformation and the Schur Complement}

To find the conditional distribution $P(f_* \mid y)$, we must mathematically isolate $f_*$ from $y$. We achieve this by applying a linear affine transformation to decorrelate the variables.

We define a transformation matrix $M$ designed to eliminate the cross-covariance terms:

$$M = \begin{bmatrix} I & \mathbf{0} \\ -K_{*y}K_{yy}^{-1} & I \end{bmatrix}, Z=\begin{bmatrix} y \\ f_* \end{bmatrix}$$

We apply this transformation matrix to our joint random vector $Z$:

$$MZ = M \begin{bmatrix} y \\ f_* \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} y \\ f_* - K_{*y}K_{yy}^{-1}y \end{bmatrix}$$

To calculate the new covariance after a linear transformation of a random vector $Z$ which follows a multivariate Gaussian distribution with Covariance $\Sigma$. We compute the covariance of this new transformed system using the property $\operatorname{Cov}(MZ) = M \Sigma M^T$:

\begin{equation}
M \Sigma M^T = \begin{bmatrix} I & \mathbf{0} \\ -K_{*y}K_{yy}^{-1} & I \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} K_{yy} & K_{y*} \\ K_{*y} & K_{**} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} I & -K_{yy}^{-1}K_{y*} \\ \mathbf{0} & I \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:z_linear_transform}
\end{equation}

Expanding the matrix multiplication step-by-step, we multiply $M$ by $\Sigma$:

$$M \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} K_{yy} & K_{y*} \\ -K_{*y}K_{yy}^{-1}K_{yy} + K_{*y} & -K_{*y}K_{yy}^{-1}K_{y*} + K_{**} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} K_{yy} & K_{y*} \\ \mathbf{0} & K_{**} - K_{*y}K_{yy}^{-1}K_{y*} \end{bmatrix}$$

Next, multiply by $M^T$:


$$M \Sigma M^T = \begin{bmatrix} K_{yy} & K_{y*} \\ \mathbf{0} & K_{**} - K_{*y}K_{yy}^{-1}K_{y*} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} I & -K_{yy}^{-1}K_{y*} \\ \mathbf{0} & I \end{bmatrix}$$

\begin{equation}
M \Sigma M^T = \begin{bmatrix} K_{yy} & -K_{yy}K_{yy}^{-1}K_{y*} + K_{y*} \\ \mathbf{0} & K_{**} - K_{*y}K_{yy}^{-1}K_{y*} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} K_{yy} & \mathbf{0} \\ \mathbf{0} & K_{**} - K_{*y}K_{yy}^{-1}K_{y*} \end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:decorrelated_covariance}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Decorrelation and Independence}

The resulting $M \Sigma M^T$ block from the \ref{eq:z_linear_transform} linear transformation gives exactly $\mathbf{0}$ in the off-diagonal covariance blocks. Since $y$ and $(f_* - K_{*y}K_{yy}^{-1}y)$ are jointly Gaussian, they become strictly statistically independent and stripped of any correlation. 

The term in the bottom right block is formally known as the \textbf{Schur Complement} of $K_{yy}$ in the joint matrix $\Sigma$:


$$S = K_{**} - K_{*y}K_{yy}^{-1}K_{y*}$$

Because $f_* - K_{*y}K_{yy}^{-1}y$ is independent of $y$, its conditional distribution given $y$ is identical to its marginal distribution:


$$f_* - K_{*y}K_{yy}^{-1}y \mid y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, S)$$

\subsection{The Result: The Conditional Predictive Distribution}

To find the distribution of our target predictive variable $f_*$ conditioned on the observed market data $y$, we simply rearrange the isolated term. Since $y$ is treated as a known constant when conditioning:

$$f_* \mid y \sim \mathcal{N}\left(K_{*y}K_{yy}^{-1}y, \ S \right)$$

Substituting our original notation back into the equation, we yield the explicit, exact formulas for the Gaussian Process predictive mean vector ($\bar{f}_*$) and predictive covariance matrix ($\Sigma_*$):

\subsubsection{Predictive Mean:}
$$\bar{f}_* = K(X_*, X) [K(X, X) + \alpha I]^{-1} y$$

\subsubsection{Predictive Covariance:}
$$\Sigma_* = K(X_*, X_*) - K(X_*, X) [K(X, X) + \alpha I]^{-1} K(X, X_*)$$

\hfill $\blacksquare$

\section{Matrix Calculus of the Log-Marginal Likelihood Gradients}
\label{sec:lml_gradients}

This derivation provides the formal proof of how gradient-based optimisers navigate the landscape and why they stall on flat ridges by computing the analytical partial derivatives of the Log-Marginal Likelihood (LML) with respect to GP covariance kernel hyperparameters $\theta_j$ (such as length-scales $\ell_K, \ell_T$, signal variance $\sigma_f^2$, or noise variance $\sigma_n^2$).

\subsection{The Setup: The Objective Function}
Let $y \in \mathbb{R}^n$ denote the vector of observed targets (Total Implied Variance) and $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ denote the input matrix. Hence, $p(y \mid X, \theta)$ is the probability of seeing the financial targets $y$, given our input coordinates $X$ and chosen hyperparameters $\theta$. The standard practice is to assume a zero-mean Gaussian Process prior (or residuals after subtracting a parametric mean prior). Thereby, the standard Multivariate Gaussian PDF is:

$$p(y \mid X, \theta) = (2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}} \vert{}K_y\vert{}^{-\frac{1}{2}} \exp \left( -\frac{1}{2} y^T K_y^{-1} y \right)$$

where $K_y = K_\theta + \sigma_n^2 I \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ represents the noisy covariance (Gram) matrix. By taking the natural logarithm, we obtain the Log-Marginal Likelihood objective function $\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \log p(y \mid X, \theta)$ that we explicitly defined in Section \ref{sec:point_estimation_std_approach}.


\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \underbrace{-\frac{1}{2} y^T K_y^{-1} y}_{\text{Data Fit Term}} \ - \ \underbrace{\frac{1}{2} \log \vert{}K_y\vert{}}_{\text{Complexity Penalty}} \ - \ \underbrace{\frac{n}{2} \log (2\pi)}_{\text{Normalization Constant}}
\label{eq:lml_objective_function}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Mathematical Prerequisites: Matrix Calculus Rules}
First, we invoke two fundamental matrix calculus identities in order to differentiate $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ with respect to a scalar hyperparameter $\theta_j$:

\subsubsection{Identity 1: Derivative of an Inverse Matrix}
Using the standard product rule, we differentiate the invertible matrix $K_y$ with respect to a scalar $\theta_j$:

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_j} (K_y K_y^{-1}) = \frac{\partial K_y}{\partial \theta_j} K_y^{-1} + K_y \frac{\partial K_y^{-1}}{\partial \theta_j} = \mathbf{0}
\label{eq:derivative_inverse_matrix}
\end{equation}

From Equation \ref{eq:derivative_inverse_matrix}, we multiply the left by $K_y^{-1}$ and isolate $\frac{\partial K_y^{-1}}{\partial \theta_j}$ to obtain:

$$\frac{\partial K_y^{-1}}{\partial \theta_j} = -K_y^{-1} \frac{\partial K_y}{\partial \theta_j} K_y^{-1}$$

\subsubsection{Identity 2: Jacobi's Formula for the Log-Determinant}
Jacobi’s formula states that for any differentiable matrix $K_y$, its derivative $\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_j} \vert{}K_y\vert{}$ is equal to $ \vert{}K_y\vert{}\text{Tr}\left( K_y^{-1} \frac{\partial K_y}{\partial \theta_j} \right)$. Specifically, when we apply the chain rule to the logarithm of the determinant, we obtain the steps \ref{eq:jacobi} through simplification.

\begin{equation}\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_j} \log \vert{}K_y\vert{} = \frac{1}{\vert{}K_y\vert{}} \frac{\partial \vert{}K_y\vert{}}{\partial \theta_j} = \frac{1}{\vert{}K_y\vert{}} \left[ \vert{}K_y\vert{} \text{Tr}\left( K_y^{-1} \frac{\partial K_y}{\partial \theta_j} \right) \right] = \text{Tr}\left( K_y^{-1} \frac{\partial K_y}{\partial \theta_j} \right)\label{eq:jacobi}\end{equation}


\subsection{The Derivation: Differentiating $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$}

We take the partial derivative of $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ with respect to $\theta_j$ component-by-component:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_j} = \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_j} \left( -\frac{1}{2} y^T K_y^{-1} y \right) - \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_j} \left( \frac{1}{2} \log \vert{}K_y\vert{} \right) - \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_j} \left( \frac{n}{2} \log (2\pi) \right)$$


\subsubsection{Step A: Differentiating the Data Fit Term}
Since $y$ does not depend on $\theta_j$, we pass the partial derivative operator inside:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_j} \left( -\frac{1}{2} y^T K_y^{-1} y \right) = -\frac{1}{2} y^T \left( \frac{\partial K_y^{-1}}{\partial \theta_j} \right) y$$

Substituting Identity \ref{eq:derivative_inverse_matrix} into the equation:

$$-\frac{1}{2} y^T \left( -K_y^{-1} \frac{\partial K_y}{\partial \theta_j} K_y^{-1} \right) y = \frac{1}{2} y^T K_y^{-1} \frac{\partial K_y}{\partial \theta_j} K_y^{-1} y$$

To simplify the term into a quadratic form, we define the predictive weights vector $\alpha = K_y^{-1} y \in \mathbb{R}^n$:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_j} \left( -\frac{1}{2} y^T K_y^{-1} y \right) = \frac{1}{2} \alpha^T \frac{\partial K_y}{\partial \theta_j} \alpha$$

\subsubsection{Step B: Differentiating the Complexity Penalty Term}

Using Identity 2 directly:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_j} \left( \frac{1}{2} \log \vert{}K_y\vert{} \right) = \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr}\left( K_y^{-1} \frac{\partial K_y}{\partial \theta_j} \right)$$

\subsubsection{Step C: Differentiating the Constant}

Since $\frac{n}{2} \log(2\pi)$ is independent of $\theta_j$, this term is equal to $0$:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_j} \left( \frac{n}{2} \log (2\pi) \right) = 0$$

\subsection{The Result: The Gradient Equation}

Combining Step A, Step B, and Step C, we obtain the exact gradient of the Log-Marginal Likelihood with respect to hyperparameter $\theta_j$:

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_j} = \frac{1}{2} y^T K_y^{-1} \frac{\partial K_y}{\partial \theta_j} K_y^{-1} y - \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr}\left( K_y^{-1} \frac{\partial K_y}{\partial \theta_j} \right)
\label{eq:gradient_equation}
\end{equation}

Or equivalently, using cyclic permutation. We can express the gradient equation using the parameter vector $\alpha = K_y^{-1} y$:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_j} = \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} \left( \left( \alpha \alpha^T - K_y^{-1} \right) \frac{\partial K_y}{\partial \theta_j} \right)$$

\hfill $\blacksquare$

\section{Analytical Derivatives of the Matérn 5/2 Kernel}
\label{sec:derivatives_matern_kernel)}

This is the mathematical derivation for the analytical derivatives of the Matérn 5/2 kernel. Here we present the exact continuous calculus required to prove that the Gaussian Process natively supports the arbitrage bounds (gradient for Calendar Arbitrage, Hessian for Butterfly Arbitrage), removing any reliance on finite-difference approximations.

\subsection{The GP Linear Operator Property}

Derived from the B.1 proof, we define the predictive mean of a Gaussian Process as:


$$\bar{f}_* = k_*^T (K_{yy})^{-1} y$$

However, now we optimise the multivariate calculus for the algorithm. Hence, we define $k_* = K(X, x_*)$, which is a row vector, as the cross-covariance vector between the training data and a single test coordinate $x_*$, and $y$ is the vector of observed targets. Because the test coordinate $x_*$ only appears inside the kernel function vector $k_*$, we can again define the pre-computed weight vector $\alpha = (K_{yy})^{-1} y$.

Because arbitrage constraints (Section 2.3) are strictly directional. We differentiate with respect to a specific dimension $i$ of the test coordinate $x_{*, i}$ (e.g., Strike $K$ or Maturity $T$):

$$\frac{\partial \bar{f}_*}{\partial x_{*, i}} = \left( \frac{\partial k_*}{\partial x_{*, i}} \right)^T \alpha$$

Similarly, the second derivative (the Hessian diagonal) required for evaluating butterfly convexity becomes:


$$\frac{\partial^2 \bar{f}_*}{\partial x_{*, i}^2} = \left( \frac{\partial^2 k_*}{\partial x_{*, i}^2} \right)^T \alpha$$

\subsection{The Matérn 5/2 Kernel and Mahalanobis Distance}
To compute the derivatives of $k_*$, by the chain rule, we must differentiate the kernel function itself. We define the anisotropic Matérn 5/2 kernel between a training point $x$ and a test point $x_*$ as:
$$k_{5/2}(r) = \sigma_f^2 \left( 1 + \sqrt{5}r + \frac{5}{3}r^2 \right) \exp(-\sqrt{5}r)$$

where $r$ is the anisotropic Mahalanobis distance. For a two-dimensional input space of Strike ($x_1 = K$) and Maturity ($x_2 = T$) with independent length-scales $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$, the Mahalanobis distance is:

$$r(x, x_*) = \sqrt{ \frac{(x_{*, 1} - x_1)^2}{\ell_1^2} + \frac{(x_{*, 2} - x_2)^2}{\ell_2^2} }$$

\subsection{The Derivation: First Derivative (The Gradient)}

We apply the chain rule to differentiate the kernel with respect to a specific spatial input coordinate $x_{*, i}$:

$$\frac{\partial k_{5/2}}{\partial x_{*, i}} = \frac{\partial k_{5/2}}{\partial r} \frac{\partial r}{\partial x_{*, i}}$$


\subsubsection{Step A: Differentiate the Kernel with Respect to Distance ($r$)}
Applying the product rule to $k_{5/2}(r)$:

$$\frac{\partial k_{5/2}}{\partial r} = \sigma_f^2 \left[\left(\sqrt{5} + \frac{10}{3}r\right) \exp(-\sqrt{5}r) - \sqrt{5} \left(1 + \sqrt{5}r + \frac{5}{3}r^2\right) \exp(-\sqrt{5}r) \right]$$

Factoring out $\sigma_f^2 \exp(-\sqrt{5}r)$ and simplifying the polynomial:

$$\frac{\partial k_{5/2}}{\partial r} = \sigma_f^2 \exp(-\sqrt{5}r) \left[ \sqrt{5} + \frac{10}{3}r - \sqrt{5} - 5r - \frac{5\sqrt{5}}{3}r^2 \right]$$

$$\therefore \frac{\partial k_{5/2}}{\partial r} = -\frac{5}{3} \sigma_f^2 (1 + \sqrt{5}r^2) \exp(-\sqrt{5}r)$$



\subsubsection{Step B: Differentiate the Distance with Respect to the Coordinate ($x_{*, i}$)}
Differentiating the Mahalanobis distance $r$ using the chain rule:

$$\frac{\partial r}{\partial x_{*, i}} = \frac{1}{2r} \cdot 2 \frac{(x_{*, i} - x_i)}{\ell_i^2} = \frac{x_{*, i} - x_i}{r \ell_i^2}$$


\subsubsection{Step C: The Complete First Derivative}
Multiplying Step A and Step B (noting that the $r$ in the denominator cancels with the $r$ in the numerator):

$$\frac{\partial k_{5/2}}{\partial x_{*, i}} = -\frac{5}{3} \sigma_f^2 (1 + \sqrt{5}r) \exp(-\sqrt{5}r) \frac{(x_{*, i} - x_i)}{\ell_i^2}$$

This first derivative evaluates the continuous local slope of the surface, natively defining the calendar arbitrage constraint when evaluated with respect to Maturity ($T$).

\subsection{The Derivation: Second Derivative (The Hessian)}
To evaluate butterfly convexity, we require the second partial derivative with respect to Strike ($K$). Let $x_{*, i} = K$. We differentiate the first derivative again using the product rule. Let the derivative of Mahalanobis distance $r$ with respect to a single test coordinate $x_{*, i}$ be defined as $D_K = \frac{x_{*, i} - x_i}{\ell_i^2}$ for simplicity. Hence, the first derivative simplifies to:

$$\frac{\partial k_{5/2}}{\partial x_{*, i}} = -\frac{5}{3} \sigma_f^2 \left[ (1 + \sqrt{5}r) \exp(-\sqrt{5}r) \right] D_K$$

By applying the product rule, we obtain:
 
$$\frac{\partial^2 k_{5/2}}{\partial x_{*, i}^2} = -\frac{5}{3} \sigma_f^2 \left[ \frac{\partial}{\partial x_{*, i}} \Big( (1 + \sqrt{5}r) \exp(-\sqrt{5}r) \Big) \cdot D_K + (1 + \sqrt{5}r) \exp(-\sqrt{5}r) \cdot \frac{\partial D_K}{\partial x_{*, i}} \right]$$

\subsubsection{Step A: Inner Chain Rule}
Let $u(r) = (1 + \sqrt{5}r) \exp(-\sqrt{5}r)$.


$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial r} = \sqrt{5} \exp(-\sqrt{5}r) - \sqrt{5}(1+\sqrt{5}r)\exp(-\sqrt{5}r) = -5r \exp(-\sqrt{5}r)$$

Multiplying by $\frac{\partial r}{\partial x_{*, i}}$:

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{*, i}} = -5r \exp(-\sqrt{5}r) \frac{x_{*, i} - x_i}{r \ell_i^2} = -5 \exp(-\sqrt{5}r) D_K$$


\subsubsection{Step B: The Second Term}
The derivative of the spatial distance component $D_K$ is simply a constant scaling factor:


$$\frac{\partial D_K}{\partial x_{*, i}} = \frac{1}{\ell_i^2}$$



\subsubsection{Step C: The Complete Second Derivative}
Substituting the components back into the product rule yields the exact analytical second derivative:


$$\frac{\partial^2 k_{5/2}}{\partial x_{*, i}^2} = -\frac{5}{3} \sigma_f^2 \left[ -5 \exp(-\sqrt{5}r) D_K^2 + (1 + \sqrt{5}r) \exp(-\sqrt{5}r) \frac{1}{\ell_i^2} \right]$$

\section{The Breeden-Litzenberger Density and Static Arbitrage}
\label{sec:breeden_litz_proof}
In this derivation, we establish the relationship between the price of a European call option and the implied risk-neutral probability density function. Essentially, by isolating the convexity term, we show that the term must remain strictly positive to avoid butterfly arbitrage.

\subsection{The Setup: Risk-Neutral Expected Payoff}

Under the fundamental theorem of asset pricing, the arbitrage-free price of a European call option $C(K, T)$ with strike $K$ and maturity $T$ is the discounted expected value of its payoff under the risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$.

Assuming a constant continuously compounded risk-free rate $r$, we can express the expectation as an integral over the risk-neutral probability density function (PDF) of the terminal asset price, denoted as $p(S_T)$:

$$C(K, T) = e^{-rT} \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[\max(S_T - K, 0)]$$

The integral can be separated into two parts, with the first ranging from $0$ to $K$ and the second from $K$ to $\infty$. Since the payoff of the first one is zero if $S_T - K < 0$, we equate the integral to 0 and proceed with the second.

\begin{equation}
C(K, T) = e^{-rT} \int_{K}^{\infty} (S_T - K) p(S_T) dS_T
\label{eq:expected_payoff_call}
\end{equation}
\subsection{The Derivation: First Derivative (The Survival Function)}

To isolate the density function $p(S_T)$, we must differentiate the integral with respect to the strike price $K$. Because the variable of differentiation $K$ appears in both the integrand and the lower limit of integration, we apply the Leibniz rule:

\TODO{should i show the leibniz rule explicitly?}

We evaluate the upper bound, $b(K) = \infty$, so $\frac{db}{dK} = 0$. The lower bound: $a(K) = K$, so $\frac{da}{dK} = 1$.
And the integrand becomes $(S_T - K)p(S_T)$.

As we evaluate the boundary terms, the upper boundary term vanishes because $\frac{db}{dK} = 0$. And the lower boundary term is evaluated at $S_T = K$, yielding $(K - K)p(K) \cdot 1 = 0$. Consequently, both terms vanish, and we are left with the integral of the partial derivative of the integrand:

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial K} = e^{-rT} \int_{K}^{\infty} \frac{\partial}{\partial K} \left[ (S_T - K) p(S_T) \right] dS_T$$

Differentiating the inside with respect to $K$ and simplifying:


\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial C}{\partial K} = e^{-rT} \int_{K}^{\infty} (-1) p(S_T) dS_T = -e^{-rT} \int_{K}^{\infty} p(S_T) dS_T
\label{eq:disc_survival_function}
\end{equation}

This first derivative represents the survival function; negative discounted risk-neutral probability that the option finishes in the money (ITM).

\subsection{The Derivation: Second Derivative (The Density)}

To prove the butterfly arbitrage constraint, we must differentiate a second time with respect to $K$. This time, $K$ only appears in the lower bound of the integral. Thereby, we simply apply the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

$$\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} = -e^{-rT} \frac{\partial}{\partial K} \int_{K}^{\infty} p(S_T) dS_T$$

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, when we differentiate an integral with respect to its lower bound, the equation simply yields the negative of the integrand evaluated at that bound:


\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} = -e^{-rT} \left( -p(K) \right) = e^{-rT} p(K)
\label{eq:initial_b_l_result}
\end{equation}
\subsection{The Result: The Butterfly Arbitrage Constraint}

After simplification, we isolate the risk-neutral probability density $p(K)$ to obtain the Breeden-Litzenberger formula:


\begin{equation}
p(K) = e^{rT} \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}
\label{eq:rearanged_b_l_result}
\end{equation}
Following Kolmogorov's foundational axioms of probability theory, a probability density function can never be negative. Therefore, it must hold true that $p(K)$ is strictly positive $p(K) \ge 0$. Additionally, because the discount factor $e^{rT}$ also can never be negative, the second partial derivative of the call price must always be positive. 

$$\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} \ge 0$$

Hence, if an interpolated surface exhibits local concavity, it directly implies the existence of a negative risk-neutral probability. This violates static butterfly constraints and makes the surface financially unreliable.

\section{Dupire’s Equation}

As per the Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing, for an option surface to be valid, there must exist a risk-neutral stochastic process (an SDE) that actually generates those prices. The Dupire equation shows that for this SDE to exist, the local variance must be strictly positive. Hence, if a butterfly arbitrage violation occurs, the local variance becomes negative, and the diffusion term $\sigma_L S_t dW_t$ becomes mathematically undefined. Consequently, rendering the option surface invalid for financial modelling. 

\subsection{The Setup: The SDE and the FPK Equation}

We assume the underlying asset $S_t$ follows a risk-neutral stochastic differential equation (SDE):

\begin{equation}
dS_t = r S_t dt + \sigma_L(S_t, t) S_t dW_t
\label{eq:sde}
\end{equation}

Putting it into words, the infinitesimal change $dS_t$ is equal to the sum of the drift term ($r S_t dt$), where $r$ is the risk-free rate and $dt$ is an infinitesimal step forward in time. And the diffusion term ($\sigma_L(S_t, t) S_t dW_t$), where $\sigma_L(S_t, t)$ represents the volatility of the asset at that particular price $S_t$ and time $t$, is scaled by the pure randomness term $dW_t$ (Brownian Motion) and the price $S_t$ of the asset. Holistically, this equation represents that the change in the stock price ($dS_t$) is equal to the predictable interest it earned ($r S_t dt$), plus or minus a random percentage shock amplified by the local volatility conditions ($\sigma_L S_t dW_t$).

Additionally, by the definitions of stochastic calculus, the transition probability density function $p(S, T)$ of the \ref{eq:sde} process must satisfy the Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov (FPK) Forward Equation:

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial p}{\partial T} = \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial S^2} \left( \sigma_L^2(S, T) S^2 p \right) - \frac{\partial}{\partial S} (r S p)
\label{eq:fpk_equation}
\end{equation}

Where $S$ serves as the dummy variable for the terminal asset price $S_T$.

\subsection{The Derivation: Differentiating the Call Price with Respect to Time}

We begin with the standard risk-neutral pricing formula for a European call, expressed as an integral over the density $p(S, T)$. This notation was previously introduced in Equation \ref{eq:expected_payoff_call}:

$$C(K, T) = e^{-rT} \int_{K}^{\infty} (S - K) p(S, T) dS$$

To find the evolution of the price over time, we differentiate both sides with respect to maturity $T$ using the product rule:

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial T} = -r e^{-rT} \int_{K}^{\infty} (S - K) p(S, T) dS + e^{-rT} \int_{K}^{\infty} (S - K) \frac{\partial p}{\partial T} dS$$

We recognise that the first term contains the original call price definition. So the equation is simply:

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial C}{\partial T} = -rC + e^{-rT} \int_{K}^{\infty} (S - K) \frac{\partial p}{\partial T} dS
\label{eq:simplified_first_derivative_call}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Substituting the FPK Equation}

In Equation \ref{eq:simplified_first_derivative_call}, we have $\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}$ (the rate at which the probability density changes over time) stuck inside an integral that is being evaluated with respect to the stock price ($dS$). This is a massive problem as we cannot integrate a time derivative with respect to a spatial variable ($S$). Because $T$ and $S$ are independent variables, we cannot evaluate this integral without knowing the explicit function $p(S,T)$ ahead of time. Hence, we make use of the FPK Equation defined in \ref{eq:fpk_equation} to convert time derivatives into spatial derivatives. By substituting the FPK Forward Equation into our $\frac{\partial p}{\partial T}$ term inside the integral, we split the integral into a diffusion term and a drift term:

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial C}{\partial T} = -rC 
+ e^{-rT} \underbrace{\int_{K}^{\infty} (S - K) \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial S^2} \left( \sigma_L^2 S^2 p \right) dS}_{\text{Diffusion Term}} 
- e^{-rT} \underbrace{\int_{K}^{\infty} (S - K) \frac{\partial}{\partial S} (r S p) dS}_{\text{Drift Term}}
\label{eq:time_derivative_eq}
\end{equation}

Firstly, we begin by evaluating the diffusion term using integration by parts. For the first pass, we let $u = (S - K)$ and $dv = \frac{\partial^2}{\partial S^2} \left( \sigma_L^2 S^2 p \right) dS$, so $du = dS$ and $v = \frac{\partial}{\partial S} \left( \sigma_L^2 S^2 p \right)$. Collecting the terms, we obtain:

$$\int_{K}^{\infty} (S - K) \frac{\partial^2}{\partial S^2} \left( \sigma_L^2 S^2 p \right) dS = 
\left[ (S - K) \frac{\partial}{\partial S} \left( \sigma_L^2 S^2 p \right) \right]_{K}^{\infty} - \int_{K}^{\infty} \frac{\partial}{\partial S} \left( \sigma_L^2 S^2 p \right) dS$$

Because of the $(S-K)$ multiplier, when we set $S$ to the lower boundary term $K$ and the upper boundary term to $\infty$, the entire boundary term becomes 0 because of density decay as $S \to \infty$. Thereby, we apply the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus to the remaining simplified equation:

\begin{equation}
- \int_{K}^{\infty} \frac{\partial}{\partial S} \left( \sigma_L^2 S^2 p \right) dS = - \left[ \sigma_L^2 S^2 p \right]_{K}^{\infty}
\label{eq:diffusion_term}
\end{equation}

Evaluating at infinity yields zero, and evaluating the term at the lower bound $K$ subtracts the term, neutralising the negative sign. Intuitively, evaluating at infinity results in zero because of the $p(S, T)$ function, which represents the likelihood of an asset to land on a price level $S$ at time $T$. The probability that an asset reaches a price level of $\infty$ is 0. Hence:

$$-\left( 0 - \sigma_L^2(K, T) K^2 p(K, T) \right) = \sigma_L^2(K, T) K^2 p(K, T)$$

Next, we evaluate the drift term using standard integration by parts. Let $u = (S - K)$, so $du = dS$. Let $dv = \frac{\partial}{\partial S} (rSp) dS$, yielding $v = rSp$.

$$\int_{K}^{\infty} (S - K) \frac{\partial}{\partial S} (rS p) dS = 
\left[ (S - K) r S p \right]_{K}^{\infty} - \int_{K}^{\infty} rS p dS$$

Now we evaluate the boundaries. At the lower bound ($S = K$), the $(S - K)$ term becomes $(K - K)$, becoming 0, and as we set ($S \to \infty$), the probability density $p(S, T)$ decays to zero faster than the polynomial grows. We are left only with the subtracted integral:

\begin{equation}
- \int_{K}^{\infty}rSp dS
\label{eq:drift_term}
\end{equation}

To express the full equation, we substitute the drift term result from \ref{eq:diffusion_term} and the diffusion term result \ref{eq:drift_term} back into the time-derivative equation from \ref{eq:time_derivative_eq}:

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial T} = -rC + e^{-rT} \left( \frac{1}{2} \sigma_L^2(K, T) K^2 p(K, T) \right) - e^{-rT} \left( - \int_{K}^{\infty} r S p dS \right)$$

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial T} = -rC + \frac{1}{2} \sigma_L^2(K, T) K^2 \left( e^{-rT} p(K, T) \right) + r e^{-rT} \int_{K}^{\infty} S p dS$$

\subsection{Connecting to Breeden-Litzenberger}
To finally isolate the local volatility term, we must rewrite the remaining components strictly in terms of the call price derivatives. First, we apply the Breeden-Litzenberger result derived in \ref{eq:initial_b_l_result} ($e^{-rT} p(K, T) = \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}$) to the diffusion term and expand the integral in the drift term by adding and subtracting $K$:

$$r e^{-rT} \int_{K}^{\infty} (S - K + K) p dS = r e^{-rT} \int_{K}^{\infty} (S - K) p dS + r K e^{-rT} \int_{K}^{\infty} p dS$$

Notice that $e^{-rT} \int_{K}^{\infty} (S - K) p dS$ is simply the Call Price $C$ from \ref{eq:expected_payoff_call}. Also recall from the Breeden-Litzenberger derivation \ref{eq:disc_survival_function} that the first derivative of the call price is the discounted survival function $\frac{\partial C}{\partial K} = -e^{-rT} \int_{K}^{\infty} p dS$. So we substitute these identities back into the expanded drift term:

$$r \left( C - K \frac{\partial C}{\partial K} \right)$$

Inserting everything back into the main equation, we obtain:

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial T} = -rC + \frac{1}{2} \sigma_L^2(K, T) K^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} + rC - rK \frac{\partial C}{\partial K}$$

The $-rC$ and $+rC$ perfectly cancel each other out, leaving:

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial T} = \frac{1}{2} \sigma_L^2(K, T) K^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} - rK \frac{\partial C}{\partial K}$$

\subsection{The Result: Dupire’s Formula}

Finally, by isolating the local variance $\sigma_L^2(K, T)$ we obtain Dupire’s final formula:


\begin{equation}
\sigma_L^2(K, T) = \frac{\frac{\partial C}{\partial T} + rK \frac{\partial C}{\partial K}}{\frac{1}{2} K^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}}
\label{eq:dupires_equation}
\end{equation}

This formula is crucial for understanding how butterfly arbitrage violations directly translate to an economically inadmissible volatility surface. Additionally, Dupire's equation converts Implied Volatility to Local Volatility for pricing exotic options (i.e., barrier, asian), and if the second derivative is negative, the local volatility becomes an imaginary number, crashing the pipeline.

\section{Mercer's Theorem and the RKHS Norm Derivation}
\label{sec:m_theorem_b6}
In this proof, we will break down a concept from pure Functional Analysis: the Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS) norm, which comes embedded in the \textit{scikit-learn} library. It represents the formal penalty which constrains the Gaussian Process Regression from creating an overly jagged surface. Hence, it is necessary to formally introduce it in this paper. 

\subsection{Definition of the Kernel Operator}
We begin by defining the kernel operator. Let $\mathcal{X}$ be a compact space and $k: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$ be a continuous, symmetric, positive semi-definite kernel function. Then, define the integral operator $T_k: L^2(\mathcal{X}) \to L^2(\mathcal{X})$ such that:

$$(T_k f)(x) = \int_{\mathcal{X}} k(x, x') f(x') dx'$$

Since we cannot decompose the kernel directly, we have to take the continuum limit of the discrete Gram matrix $K \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$, thereby obtaining the linear integral operator $T_k: L^2(\mathcal{X}) \to L^2(\mathcal{X})$. Essentially upgrading a discrete matrix $K$ to its continuous counterpart $T_k$ to be able to decompose the kernel by using Mercer's Theorem.

\subsection{Mercer's Theorem (Eigen-decomposition)}
By Mercer’s Theorem, $T_k$ admits an orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions $\{\phi_i\}_{i=1}^{\infty}$ and a corresponding set of non-negative eigenvalues $\{\lambda_i\}_{i=1}^{\infty}$ ordered such that $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge 0$. Intuitively, Mercer's Theorem guarantees that we can break our integral operator $T_k$ into an infinite set of perfectly independent eigenfunctions and sort them by their importance (eigenvalues). So, the eigenvalue problem is defined as:

$$\int_{\mathcal{X}} k(x, x') \phi_i(x') dx' = \lambda_i \phi_i(x)$$

By Mercer's Theorem, we can express our Matern kernel as a uniformly convergent series:

$$k(x, x') = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \lambda_i \phi_i(x) \phi_i(x')$$

\subsection{Construction of the RKHS ($\mathcal{H}_k$)}
Having deconstructed the kernel into eigenfunctions and eigenvalues, we pick any two basis functions to construct the RKHS norm. Let $\mathcal{H}_k$ be the function space defined by the span of the eigenfunctions, formally $\mathcal{H}_k = \text{span}\{\phi_i\}_{i=1}^{\infty}$. Any function $f, g \in \mathcal{H}_k$ can be expressed as a linear combination of these basis functions:

$$f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} c_i \phi_i(x)$$
$$g(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} d_i \phi_i(x)$$


where $c_i, d_i \in \mathbb{R}$.

\subsection{Verification of the Reproducing Property}
To prove $\mathcal{H}_k$ is a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space, we must show that $\langle f, k(\cdot, x) \rangle_{\mathcal{H}_k} = f(x)$. Where, by definition, the inner product space for $\mathcal{H}_k$ is $\langle f, g \rangle_{\mathcal{H}_k} = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \frac{c_i d_i}{\lambda_i}$. Contingent on the fact that $c_i, d_i$ must decay sufficiently fast such that the sum converges. Firstly, we express the kernel function $k(\cdot, x)$ for a fixed $x$ in terms of the basis functions. Let $d_i = \lambda_i \phi_i(x)$:


$$k(\cdot, x) = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} (\lambda_i \phi_i(x)) \phi_i(\cdot)$$

We substitute $c_i$ from $f(x)$ and $d_i$ from $k(\cdot, x)$ into the inner product definition and obtain:

$$\langle f, k(\cdot, x) \rangle_{\mathcal{H}_k} = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \frac{c_i (\lambda_i \phi_i(x))}{\lambda_i}$$

As a result, the $\lambda_i$ terms cancel out:

$$\langle f, k(\cdot, x) \rangle_{\mathcal{H}_k} = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} c_i \phi_i(x)$$

By the definition of $f(x)$ established priorly, this evaluates to:

$$\langle f, k(\cdot, x) \rangle_{\mathcal{H}_k} = f(x)$$

Thus, proving that $\mathcal{H}_k$ is indeed a valid RKHS.

\subsection{Derivation of the RKHS Norm}
The RKHS norm of the function $f \in \mathcal{H}_k$ can be found by its inner product with itself. Setting $g(x) = f(x)$, we imply that $d_i = c_i$:

\begin{equation}
\vert{}\vert{}f\vert{}\vert{}_{\mathcal{H}_k}^2 = \langle f, f \rangle_{\mathcal{H}_k}
\label{eq:rkhs_norm}
\end{equation}

$$\vert{}\vert{}f\vert{}\vert{}_{\mathcal{H}_k}^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \frac{c_i c_i}{\lambda_i}$$

$$\vert{}\vert{}f\vert{}\vert{}_{\mathcal{H}_k}^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \frac{c_i^2}{\lambda_i}$$

It is important to note that the definition of the RKHS norm from Equation \ref{eq:rkhs_norm} cannot be evaluated by the computer because of the infinite sum of eigenvalues ($\sum \frac{c_i^2}{\lambda_i}$). Instead, it uses the Representer Theorem, which states that the infinite-dimensional RKHS norm is perfectly equal to a finite matrix calculation:

$$\vert{}\vert{}f\vert{}\vert{}_{\mathcal{H}_k}^2 = y^T K_y^{-1} y$$

This formulation can be found in the data fit term in Equation \ref{eq:lml_objective_function} of Appendix \ref{sec:lml_gradients} for the Log-Marginal Likelihood.

\subsection{Application to the Gaussian Process Loss Functional}
Under Tikhonov regularisation (and by extension, the negative log marginal likelihood of a Gaussian Process), the objective functional $J(f)$ over $N$ observations is:

$$J(f) = \sum_{j=1}^{N} (y_j - f(x_j))^2 + \sigma_n^2 \vert{}\vert{}f\vert{}\vert{}_{\mathcal{H}_k}^2$$

Where, practically, $\sum_{j=1}^{N} (y_j - f(x_j))^2$ is the data error term and $\sigma_n^2 \vert{}\vert{}f\vert{}\vert{}_{\mathcal{H}_k}^2$ is the RKHS norm penalty. Finally, by substituting the derived norm into the objective functional $J(f)$, we obtain the final result:

$$J(f) = \sum_{j=1}^{N} \left( y_j - \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} c_i \phi_i(x_j) \right)^2 + \sigma_n^2 \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \frac{c_i^2}{\lambda_i}$$\

\section{Fisher Information Matrix (FIM) for a Gaussian Process}
\label{sec:fim_gp}
Because for most of the option data, the optimiser stumbles across a flat ridge in the LML surface. We need to understand how confident our model is in the hyperparameters it chose. Thus, this proof is foundational to the L-BFGS-B optimisation chapter because it quantifies our uncertainty about our model.

\subsection{The Log-Marginal Likelihood}
We begin by setting up the objective function $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ of the Log-Marginal Likelihood we defined in Section \label{sec:point_estimation_std_approach}.

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{2} y^T K^{-1} y - \frac{1}{2} \log \vert{}K\vert{} - \frac{N}{2} \log(2\pi)$$

\subsection{The Score Function}
To find how the likelihood changes when we change a single hyperparameter (like a lengthscale $\theta_i$), we take the first partial derivative to obtain the score function, which we derived in Equation \ref{eq:gradient_equation}. 

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_i} = \frac{1}{2} y^T K^{-1} \frac{\partial K}{\partial \theta_i} K^{-1} y - \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr}\left( K^{-1} \frac{\partial K}{\partial \theta_i} \right)$$

Intuitively, the first term of the score function measures how changing the kernel affects the data fit, and the second term (the trace) measures how changing the kernel affects the RKHS norm.

\subsection{The Second Derivative (The Hessian)}

To obtain the Fisher Information Matrix, which is defined as the negative expected value of the second derivative (the Hessian matrix). Hence, we differentiate the score function, this time with respect to a second hyperparameter $\theta_j$. For simplicity, let $K_i = \frac{\partial K}{\partial \theta_i}$ and $K_j = \frac{\partial K}{\partial \theta_j}$ to save space. We apply the product rule and obtain:

$$-\frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_j} \text{Tr}(K^{-1} K_i) = -\frac{1}{2} \text{Tr}\left( -K^{-1} K_j K^{-1} K_i + K^{-1} K_{ij} \right)$$

Differentiating the Data Term (Quadratic Form):


\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta_j} \left( y^T K^{-1} K_i K^{-1} y \right) = \frac{1}{2} y^T \left( -K^{-1} K_j K^{-1} K_i K^{-1} + K^{-1} K_{ij} K^{-1} - K^{-1} K_i K^{-1} K_j K^{-1} \right) y
\label{eq:quadratic_form_fim}
\end{equation}


\subsection{The Expectation}
We define the Fisher Information Matrix $\mathcal{I}_{ij}$ as the negative Expectation over all possible market observations $y$:

$$\mathcal{I}_{ij} = -\mathbb{E}_y \left[ \frac{\partial^2 \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_i \partial \theta_j} \right]$$

In order to evaluate the expectation of the massive quadratic form from Equation \ref{eq:quadratic_form_fim}. We use a trace trick from linear algebra. For any matrix $A$, the expectation of a quadratic form is $\mathbb{E}[y^T A y] = \text{Tr}(A \cdot \mathbb{E}[yy^T])$.
Because $\mathbb{E}[yy^T]$ is simply the covariance matrix $K$, the rule simplifies to:

$$\mathbb{E}[y^T A y] = \text{Tr}(A K)$$

After applying the trace trick and simplifying, we are left with:

$$-\mathbb{E} [\text{Quadratic Term}] = -\frac{1}{2} \text{Tr}\left( \left( -2 K^{-1} K_j K^{-1} K_i K^{-1} + K^{-1} K_{ij} K^{-1} \right) K \right)$$

As we distribute the coefficient and matrix $K$ across both terms, we can safely cancel out $K^{-1}$ at the end of the brackets with the $K$ from the trace trick:

$$-\mathbb{E} [\text{Quadratic Term}] = \text{Tr}(K^{-1} K_j K^{-1} K_i) - \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr}(K^{-1} K_{ij})$$

\subsection{The Result: Fisher Information Matrix}
After simplifying, we now apply the negative expectation to the trace term. Because the trace contains no $y$ variables, meaning it is entirely deterministic, the expectation does nothing. We just flip the sign:

$$-\mathbb{E} [\text{Trace Term}] = -\frac{1}{2} \text{Tr}(K^{-1} K_j K^{-1} K_i) + \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr}(K^{-1} K_{ij})$$

Finally, we collect the terms and cancel out the second cross-derivatives ($\frac{1}{2} \text{Tr}(K^{-1} K_{ij})$) to obtain the Fisher Information matrix element $\mathcal{I}_{ij}$:

$$\mathcal{I}_{ij} = \left[ \text{Tr}(K^{-1} K_j K^{-1} K_i) - \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr}(K^{-1} K_{ij}) \right] + \left[ -\frac{1}{2} \text{Tr}(K^{-1} K_j K^{-1} K_i) + \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr}(K^{-1} K_{ij}) \right]$$

$$\mathcal{I}_{ij} = \frac{1}{2} \text{Tr} \left( K^{-1} \frac{\partial K}{\partial \theta_i} K^{-1} \frac{\partial K}{\partial \theta_j} \right)$$

We have just mathematically proven that the curvature of our optimisation landscape is entirely dependent on how the covariance matrix $K$ reacts to changes in its hyperparameters ($\frac{\partial K}{\partial \theta}$). Because our Matérn kernel is fully differentiable, this FIM is guaranteed to exist and be well-behaved, ensuring that our L-BFGS-B optimiser will reliably converge to the safest possible volatility surface without crashing.

\hfill $\blacksquare$

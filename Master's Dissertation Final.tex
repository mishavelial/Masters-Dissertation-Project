\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Context and Motivation}
Accurate modelling of the implied volatility surface is central to modern quantitative finance. Volatility surfaces are required for pricing, hedging, calibration, and risk management, particularly in index option markets such as SPX. At the same time, nonparametric machine learning methods, and Gaussian Processes in particular, offer a flexible framework for modelling nonlinear structure with uncertainty quantification.

This dissertation is motivated by the need for a reliable implied-volatility surface for pricing and risk management. As financial data is naturally messy, Gaussian Process regression is a flexible yet reliable solution that has the potential to interpolate this sparse and noisy volatility data smoothly. However, the practical performance of the regression is heavily dependent on the estimation of unstable kernel hyperparameters. This paper studies whether the Bayesian approach to those hyperparameters gives a more reliable surface.

\section{Problem Statement}

The problem considered in this dissertation is the failure of the deterministic point estimation algorithm in weakly identifiable topological landscapes of the Log-Marginal Likelihood for Gaussian Process volatility surface models. Under noisy and chaotic market data, the L-BFGS-B algorithm suffers from arbitrary hyperparameter configurations, depending on the initialisation coordinate. Because the ridge at the bottom of the LML surface is flat, any point yields near-equivalent log-marginal likelihood values but results in different predictive surfaces and pricing outputs.

\section{Research Objectives and Hypothesis}
\textbf{The main research question is:}
\begin{quote}
How does the weak identifiability of Gaussian Process hyperparameters impact the out-of-sample predictive accuracy of the implied volatility surface, and can Bayesian inference via Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC/NUTS) resolve this instability to improve pricing performance while maintaining arbitrage-free constraints?
\end{quote}
This dissertation adopts the central hypothesis that flat ridges in the log-marginal likelihood topology cause deterministic optimisers to fail, and that replacing point estimation with a full posterior distribution over hyperparameters will improve out-of-sample prediction accuracy with only a minimal and acceptable trade-off in localised butterfly arbitrage violations. 

\section{Dissertation Structure}
The dissertation is organised into eight chapters. Chapter 2 introduces the financial and arbitrage foundations of the implied volatility surface. Chapter 3 develops the Gaussian Process framework and point-estimation approach. Chapter 4 empirically identifies the weak-identifiability pathology. Chapter 5 introduces Bayesian hyperparameter inference using HMC/NUTS. Chapter 6 describes the data and empirical design. Chapter 7 presents the results, and Chapter 8 concludes with implications, limitations, and future work.

\chapter{Financial Foundations and Constraints}

\section{The Implied Volatility Surface}
Before discussing the financial constraints, we should introduce the object to which they will apply: the implied volatility surface. The surface is a three-dimensional graph that maps the volatility of option contracts across several maturities and strikes for a single asset. Specifically, a function mapping of an option’s strike $K$ and time to maturity $T$ can be defined as $(K, T) \mapsto \sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}(K,T)$ across available strikes and maturities. The annualised volatility figure is derived from the inverted Black-Scholes-Merton formula to standardise absolute option prices. This standardisation enables comparison of an option across different strikes and maturities.

Importantly, the Black-Scholes implied volatility (denoted as $\sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}(K,T)$) is the unique volatility parameter that, when used in the B-S model, yields the option’s exact market price. It acts as a forward-looking metric, pricing in the market consensus on expected risk and uncertainty rather than past price movements. In practice, it reflects the premium or compensation that traders demand today for bearing risk.

This expected risk is difficult to evaluate by only looking at absolute metrics such as spot and options prices. Hence, we should take a more relative approach in order to gauge this uncertainty, which implies constructing log-moneyness, defined as $k = \log(K/F_0)$, where $F_0$ denotes the asset’s forward price at time zero. By using logarithms, we produce a stable and normalised representation of the volatility surface, independent of the underlying asset’s price dynamics.

\paragraph{The Role of SPX Options.}
For practical purposes, the underlying asset for this empirical evaluation is the S\&P 500 Index (SPX), which tracks the performance of the largest U.S. Fortune 500 companies. The SPX derivatives serve as the primary benchmark for global equity derivative markets. This is due to a couple of reasons: the SPX being exceptionally liquid across many strikes and maturities, possessing a European-style contract type without the option of early exercise, and abundant documentation. Additionally, the SPX surface possesses a highly pronounced structural downside skew. This extreme asymmetry makes SPX options a highly interesting testing ground for evaluating Bayesian hyperparameter inference and surface modelling.

\section{Static Arbitrage Constraints}
\label{subsec:static-arbitrage}
Before introducing the Gaussian Process model, we must specify the constraints that will ensure the implied volatility surface is not only mathematically, but also financially and legally sound. The two static arbitrage constraints that are of most importance for this purpose are the \emph{calendar arbitrage} and the \emph{butterfly arbitrage}. These constraints allow for the surface to have a valid risk-neutral probability density function. In turn, this makes it financially impossible if it implies the existence of a portfolio with a non-negative payoff and a negative cost. Hence, a mathematically smooth and stable interpolant is not sufficient; it must also be economically admissible.

\paragraph{Risk-neutral pricing framework.}
Another important topic of discussion that formalises the aforementioned is a framework central to asset pricing: the risk-neutral principle. Strictly, if $X_T$ is a payoff at maturity $T$, then its time-$0$ value is

\[X_0 = e^{-rT}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[X_T],\]

where $r$ is the continuously compounded risk-free rate and $\mathbb{Q}$ is the risk-neutral measure. For a European call option with strike $K$, the payoff is

\[(S_T - K)^+ = \max(S_T - K, 0),\]

so its arbitrage-free price is

\[C(K,T) = e^{-rT}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[(S_T - K)^+].\]

This pricing identity is the starting point for both static-arbitrage conditions: calendar arbitrage is linked to the option price rising over time, while butterfly arbitrage is linked to the payoff profile having a non-negative probability density. 

However, these conditions are difficult to implement through pure implied volatility (\(\sigma _{BS}\)), which makes static arbitrage constraints highly non-linear and mathematically intractable. A paper on SVI Surfaces (\citet{gatheral2014arbitrage}) pioneered the idea of shifting to total variance space \(w(k, \tau)\), which linearises the no-arbitrage conditions, making them far easier to enforce mathematically. The relationship of implied volatility to total variance is straightforward:

\[w(k,T) = \sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}^2(k,T)\,T\]

where $k = \log(K/S_0)$ is log-moneyness. We prefer the total variance space for two reasons. Firstly, it removes the trivial $\sqrt{T}$ scaling present in implied volatility, thereby making IV surfaces across different maturities comparable on a uniform scale. The trivial proof will not be discussed here. Secondly, the calendar arbitrage conditions can be naturally expressed as monotonicity conditions on $w(k,T)$, while butterfly arbitrage conditions can be checked after converting the fitted surface back into call prices.

In other words, the total variance space serves as a smooth framework that allows us to conveniently assert the financial constraints, which we will discuss further.

%--------------------------------------------------------- calendar arb
\subsection{Calendar Arbitrage}
\label{subsubsec:calendar-arbitrage}

As described by Gatheral, calendar arbitrage arises from the calendar spread option strategy, which involves two options with the same strike price but different maturities. An arbitrage opportunity arises when the longer-maturity option has a lower total variance than the shorter-maturity one. This makes the near-month contract overpriced, which in turn could be shorted, and the far-month contract underpriced. It goes without saying that this applies only to European options or non-dividend-paying American options, which we will assume from now on. In practical terms, the total variance should increase monotonically with respect to time maturity \(T\).\citep{gatheral2006volatility}

\paragraph{Link to total variance.}
In total variance terms, the calendar arbitrage constraint becomes a monotonicity condition in maturity:
\[\frac{\partial w}{\partial T}(k,T) \geq 0.\]
The $w(k,T)$ represents the cumulative variance up to time $T$, the constraint implies that with every step, the variance should not decrease. In other words, the uncertainty should accumulate over time and never decrease; otherwise, it would create an arbitrage opportunity.

\paragraph{Numerical implementation.}
Numerically implementing the constraint in total variance space is also easier; the condition is checked on the discrete maturity grid $T_1, \dots, T_n$ using first differences. 

\[\frac{w(k_i,T_{j+1}) - w(k_i,T_j)}{T_{j+1} - T_j} < 0\]

This provides a simple, transparent numerical proxy for the continuous condition $\partial w/\partial T \geq 0$.

\begin{proposition}[Calendar monotonicity]
For each fixed strike \(K\), the map \(T\mapsto C(K,T)\) is nondecreasing. Consequently, the total implied variance

\[w(k,T)=\sigma_{\mathrm{imp}}^2(k,T)\,T\]

is nondecreasing in \(T\) for each fixed log-moneyness \(k\); in particular, if \(w\) is differentiable,
\[\frac{\partial w}{\partial T}(k,T) \geq 0.\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let \(0<T_1<T_2\). No-arbitrage implies \(C(K,T_2)\ge C(K,T_1)\). Since the Black--Scholes call price is strictly increasing in total variance, the result is as follows.
\end{proof}

%--------------------------------------------------------- butterfly arb
\subsection{Butterfly Arbitrage}
\label{subsubsec:butterfly-arbitrage}

Butterfly arbitrage originates from the butterfly spread option strategy, which involves four options with staggered strike prices. It entails purchasing a call at a low strike price $K_1$, selling two calls at a middle strike price $K_2$, and buying a call at a high strike price $K_3$ (where $K_3 - K_2 = K_2 - K_1$). This arbitrage indicates that the implied probability distribution of the asset is negative and invalid, often due to an overly steep or improperly smoothed IV surface during interpolation. Consequently, the position's cost can become negative, meaning the trader profits risk-free without owning the underlying asset.

\paragraph{Intuition.}
A natural way to test this constraint is to examine the local curvature; the curve should always be convex, so the middle strike should not lie above the average of the neighbouring strikes. In other words, if the middle option price is too high relative to the neighbouring points, an arbitrage opportunity exists. The connection to the fundamental Breeden--Litzenberger theorem is that a sufficiently tight butterfly spread is a finite-difference estimate of the second derivative of the call price curve with respect to strike $K$.

As derived by Breeden and Litzenberger (1978, p. 627)\citep{breeden1978prices}, the pricing function $P(M,T)$ is “analogous to a density function,” and in Section II, page 627, the authors derive: 

\[ \lim_{\Delta M \to 0}\frac{P(M,T;\Delta M)}{\Delta M} = \left.\frac{\partial^2 c(X,T)}{\partial X^2}\right|_{X=M}, \]

\paragraph{Relationship to total variance.}
As we just proved, butterfly arbitrage can be naturally stated in terms of call prices, unlike calendar arbitrage, which is inherently a monotonicity condition on the total variance across maturities. However, since the fitted model is expressed in the total-variance space, the implied total variance space must be converted back to call prices before assessing the convexity condition. Simply, this is done using the Black--Scholes formula:

\[ C_{\mathrm{BS}}(S,K,T,r,\sigma) = S\,N(d_1) - K e^{-rT}N(d_2),\]

where

\[d_1 = \frac{\log(S/K) + (r + \tfrac12\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}. \]

\paragraph{Numerical implementation.}
The numerical implementation mirrors the mathematical definition. We use the Breeden-Litzenberger condition, where the risk-neutral probability equals the second partial derivative with respect to strike $\partial^2 C/\partial K^2\geq 0$. This is done by implementing the central finite-difference approximation of option prices across a grid to detect negative probabilities, which would then be counted as a butterfly arbitrage. 

\paragraph{Derivation.}
The derivation comes from the proven Breeden--Litzenberger result \citep{breeden1978prices}. Starting from the risk-neutral pricing formula for a European call, which can be written in the expanded form of the Expected Value $\E$.
\[ C(K,T) = e^{-rT}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[(S_T-K)^+] = e^{-rT}\int_K^\infty (s-K)f_{S_T}(s)\,ds.\]
Differentiating once with respect to strike leaves us with the discounted survival function up to a discount factor under $\Q$

\[-\frac{\partial C}{\partial K}(K,T)= e^{-rT}\int_K^\infty f_{S_T}(s)\,ds, \]
Differentiating the function again yields the Breeden--Litzenberger result \citep{breeden1978prices}:


\[\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}(K,T) = e^{-rT}f_{S_T}(K),\]

so that

\[f_{S_T}(K) = e^{rT}\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}.\]

Because a density must be non-negative, the call price must satisfy

\[\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}(K,T) \geq 0.\]

This is the continuous convexity condition underlying butterfly arbitrage. A violation means that the implied density is negative somewhere, which is not financially admissible.

\section{The Parametric Prior}
In nonparametric machine learning models like Gaussian Processes, setting a mean function lets us include financial knowledge before seeing any data. Rather than making the Gaussian Process learn the overall shape of the implied volatility surface from nothing, we use a parametric prior.
We define the prior distribution as:

\begin{equation}
w(K,T) = T\left(|a| + |b|(\log K)^2 + c \log K\right).
\label{parametric_prior}
\end{equation}

The mean function uses a quadratic polynomial in log-moneyness, which is heavily based on the Stochastic Volatility-Inspired (SVI) parametrisation \citep{gatheral2014arbitrage}. By avoiding the full five-parameter SVI, which is difficult to optimise, the resulting model is essentially a parabolic curve in log-moneyness. Where $(\log K)^2$ provides the characteristic convex smile, $\log K$ is the volatility skew, and $T$ scales it across maturities.

Originally, the standard Gatheral's raw SVI formula is indifferent to the definition of variance, which can never be negative. Hence, the inclusion of absolute values for $a$ and $b$. Alternatively, a standard unconstrained optimiser (e.g., Nelder-Mead or L-BFGS-B) might blindly test for negative values, causing the total variance to become negative and crash the pricing engine. By incorporating |a| and |b|, a differentiable barrier is created where the optimiser can search anywhere from $-\infty$ to $\infty$ freely while mathematically guaranteeing a positive total variance.

Finally, this setup also works well with the tail behaviour suggested by the moment-based constraints model. In other words, deep OTM options encode the tails of the risk-neutral distribution; their prices must remain consistent with that distribution. The prior is not just smoothing the data, but it also encodes a theoretically allowed tail growth rate for the implied variance, based on which moments of the underlying asset are finite. The far OTM smile should match the asymptotic limits set by Roger Lee’s moment formula bounds \citep{lee2004moment}. Even though there is little real-option data far out of the money, this structured prior keeps the extrapolated total variance within economic bounds and follows the theoretical maximum growth rates of the volatility smile at extreme strikes.

\chapter{Gaussian Processes in Financial Modelling}

\section{GP Regression Framework}
We can formally define the Gaussian Process (GP) as a collection of random variables, any of which possesses a joint Gaussian distribution. This way, it provides a non-parametric Bayesian framework for inferring other functions. A GP is specified by its mean function $m(\mathbf{x})$ and its covariance function, or kernel, $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')$. We denote the Gaussian Process prior as:

\[f(\mathbf{x}) \sim \mathcal{GP}(m(\mathbf{x}), k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}'))\]

The mean function, defined as $m(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{x})]$, gives the GP the global topology of the function space. More casually, it represents the prior belief or an assumption about the distribution of an unknown function. It is set to dictate the overall smoothness, behaviour and interpolation of the unknown function. 
In standard machine learning literature, the mean function $m(\mathbf{x})$ is often set to zero for algebraic convenience; however, a financial surface is inherently structurally asymmetrical. This asymmetry can be observed in features such as the persistent equity skew. Hence, the mean function needs careful tuning in order to safely extrapolate quotes in the sparsely populated deep out-of-the-money tails. To address this, the framework explicitly defines $m(\mathbf{x})$ using the parametric prior formulated in Equation \ref{parametric_prior}:

\[m(\mathbf{x}) = T(|a| + |b|k^2 + c k)\]

By substituting this SVI-inspired formulation into the GP mean function, the global structural constraints and theoretical asymptotic tail bounds are embedded directly in the prior distribution. Consequently, the learning burden shifts: the GP is no longer required to infer the macroeconomic shape of the smile from raw data. Instead, it is solely tasked with modelling the residuals. The residuals represent the localised, non-parametric deviations of the observed market data from the parametric mean. These residuals are governed by the covariance kernel, $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \mathbb{E}[(f(\mathbf{x}) - m(\mathbf{x}))(f(\mathbf{x}') - m(\mathbf{x}'))]$. Essentially, this is the covariance between the process values at $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{x'}$, and $k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x'})$ measures how these residuals vary together. The kernel describes how points on the volatility surface are related. It controls how smooth the surface is and how quickly the effect of one data point fades as you move across strike and term.

To formalise the regression model, assume a dataset of $n$ noisy total variance observations $\mathbf{y} = \{y_1, \dots, y_n\}^{\top}$ evaluated at input locations $X = \{\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_n\}$. The observation model is given by:

\[y_i = f(\mathbf{x}_i) + \epsilon_i\]

where $\epsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_n^2)$ represents independent, identically distributed Gaussian noise. In finance, the noise variance term ($\sigma_n^2$) is essential for accounting for market microstructure noise, discrete tick sizes, and the effects of the bid-ask spread.

Given a set of test inputs $X_*$, the joint distribution of the training targets $\mathbf{y}$ and the predictive function values $\mathbf{f}_*$ under the GP prior is multivariate Gaussian:

\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{y} \\ \mathbf{f}_* \end{bmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N} \left( \begin{bmatrix} m(X) \\ m(X_*) \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} K(X, X) + \sigma_n^2 I & K(X, X_*) \\ K(X_*, X) & K(X_*, X_*) \end{bmatrix} \right)
\label{joint_gaussian}
\end{equation}

where $K(X, X)$ is the $n \times n$ Gram matrix evaluated on the training inputs, $K(X, X_*)$ is the $n \times n_*$ cross-covariance matrix between the training $X$ and test $X_*$ inputs, and $I$ is the identity matrix. The statement \ref{joint_gaussian} asserts that by combining the observed training inputs and unknown test inputs together, the resulting joint distribution is Gaussian under the GP model. Importantly, the left block is the covariance between observed training inputs, plus the noise $\sigma^2I$ term, which indicates that the observed training targets $\mathbf{y}$ are noisy versions of the underlying function values. In this case, the model allows for noise rather than treating the observed data as exact realisations of the latent function. 

By conditioning this joint prior on the observed market data $\mathbf{y}$, we derive the predictive posterior distribution $\mathbf{f}_* | X, \mathbf{y}, X_* \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_*, \Sigma_*)$. Equivalently, the above expression states that once we know the training outputs, we can update the joint Gaussian to get a new Gaussian distribution for the unknown test outputs. This is a standard conditioning step in GP regression. The exact matrix equations for the predictive mean and predictive covariance are:

\[\boldsymbol{\mu}_* = m(X_*) + K(X_*, X)[K(X, X) + \sigma_n^2 I]^{-1}(\mathbf{y} - m(X))\]

The predictive mean equation states that the forecast at the new points is the sum of the prior mean and a correction term, which is the covariance between the test and training inputs, weighted by the inverse of the covariance between training inputs, multiplied by the GP deviations from the mean function.

\[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_* = K(X_*, X_*) - K(X_*, X)[K(X, X) + \sigma_n^2 I]^{-1}K(X, X_*)\]

The predictive covariance formula provides the model with the uncertainty after observing the data, where $K(X_*, X_*)$ is the prior uncertainty at the test points minus the part explained by the training data. In practical terms, uncertainty is minimised in dense regions and remains higher where the model must extrapolate.

\section{The Mat\'ern 5/2 Kernel}
While the mean function determines the overall shape of the volatility surface, the covariance kernel governs the local shape at discrete points and the interpolative behaviour of the residuals. The choice of kernel cannot be arbitrary; in financial applications, a covariance function should satisfy strict structural requirements dictated by arbitrage pricing theory. In this work, we adopt an anisotropic Matérn 5/2 kernel; other common kernels (RBF, Matérn 3/2, Rational Quadratic) are viable in general-purpose modelling but have features that are discussed briefly below, which make them less suitable for the present arbitrage-constrained framework. \TODO{add a brief explanation to why other kernels are not suitable}

The covariance kernel is a function of two inputs, $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{x}'$. We define two distinct input vectors: $\mathbf{x} = (k, T)^{\top}$ and $\mathbf{x}' = (k', T')^{\top}$. Furthermore, to account for the fundamentally different dynamics along the strike and term-structure dimensions, we define the anisotropic Mahalanobis distance $r$ using independent length-scales $\ell_k$ and $\ell_T$:

$$r(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \sqrt{\frac{(k - k')^2}{\ell_k^2} + \frac{(T - T')^2}{\ell_T^2}}$$

The Matérn $5/2$ covariance function is then formally defined as:

$$k_{5/2}(r) = \sigma_f^2 \left( 1 + \sqrt{5}r + \frac{5}{3}r^2 \right) \exp(-\sqrt{5}r)$$

where $\sigma_f^2$ represents the signal variance, controlling the vertical amplitude of the latent Gaussian Process.
\paragraph{Justification and Differentiability Constraints}

The Matérn class of covariance functions is characterised by its versatility and smoothness parameter $\nu$, which allows for explicit control of the underlying random field. This is especially useful for Implied Volatility surfaces, as they require a twice-differentiable kernel to extract arbitrage-free risk-neutral probabilities from the surface. As established in standard Gaussian Process literature (\citet{rasmussen2006gp}), the sample paths of a GP with a Matérn kernel are $m$ times mean-square differentiable if and only if $\nu > m$. Consequently, setting $\nu = 5/2$ guarantees that the resulting latent function space is exactly twice mean-square differentiable ($\mathcal{C}^2$).

This exact $\mathcal{C}^2$ regularity is almost a requirement when modelling implied volatility surfaces. The popular Squared Exponential (or Gaussian) kernel, which is the case when $\nu$ approaches infinity, gives sample paths that are infinitely smooth and analytic. However, this level of smoothness does not match real financial markets, since it over-smooths local volatility spikes and misses the small-scale frictions seen in options trading. The surface becomes silky-smooth, which is great for extrapolating for far OTM options, but pricing in between quotes is too restrictive. On the other hand, if $\nu = 3/2$ or $\nu = 1/2$, the resulting functions are only once differentiable or not differentiable at all, as in the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process.

As mentioned before, the reason for exactly twice-differentiable sample paths is linked to the extraction of the market-implied risk-neutral density. As derived from the Breeden-Litzenberger theorem (\citet{breeden1978prices}), the risk-neutral probability density $p(K,T)$ is strictly proportional to the second partial derivative of the call price with respect to the strike:

$$p(K,T) = e^{rT} \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}$$

The total variance surface $w(k,T)$, mapped by the GP, needs to be inverted using the Black-Scholes formula to calculate call prices. The Black-Scholes model needs a volatility input to produce call prices. Once we know $w(k,T)$, we convert it to IV and price the option. For this to be an arbitration-free, the underlying interpolant must allow stable second-order differentiation. On the other hand, if the sample paths are not twice differentiable, the finite-difference methods used for computing $\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2}$ can become unstable, undefined or show singularities, which could falsely indicate butterfly arbitrage violations.

In brief, the Matérn kernel with $\nu = 5/2$ specification offers an ideal balance between smoothness and restriction. It creates smooth curves while still giving the algorithm enough flexibility to accommodate localised peaks and valleys. Additionally, because $\nu$ is a fraction, it involves simpler polynomial and exponential terms that reduce cost complexity and prevent mathematical instability.

\section{The Standard Approach: Point Estimation}

The standard paradigm for training a Gaussian Process in applied machine learning is to select the hyperparameters $\theta$ that maximise the probability of the observed data. This is achieved by maximising the log-marginal likelihood (LML). Conditioning the joint Gaussian prior on the observed target vector $\mathbf{y}$, we define the log-marginal likelihood as:

\begin{equation}
\log p(\mathbf{y} | X, \theta) = -\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}^\top K_y^{-1}\mathbf{y} - \frac{1}{2}\log|K_y| - \frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi)
\label{lml_conditioning}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
K_y = K_\theta + \sigma_n^2 I
\label{noise_covariance_matrix}
\end{equation}

represents the noisy covariance matrix. 


To understand why hyperparameter optimisation often fails in the financial setting, we need to deconstruct the LML function. The kernel hyperparameters are estimated by maximising the Logarithm of Marginal Likelihood (LML), which balances data fit and model complexity. In practical terms, the LML balances model flexibility against data interpolation. This can be seen by decomposing the formula into three distinct theoretical components:

$$\log p(\mathbf{y} | X, \theta) = \underbrace{-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}^\top K_y^{-1}\mathbf{y}}_{\text{Data Fit}} - \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\log|K_y|}_{\text{Complexity Penalty}} - \underbrace{\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi)}_{\text{Normalization Constant}}$$

The fundamental geometry of LML is a balance between the first two terms:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{The Data Fit (Mahalanobis Distance):} The term $-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}^\top K_y^{-1}\mathbf{y}$ measures how well do the observed outputs align with the covariance structure, calculated via the Mahalanobis distance between the observed data and the GP prior mean (which is zeroed out here for the residuals). Additionally, $K_y^{-1}$ reweights the data based on what points should co-vary, not just when the residuals are small but also when those residuals fit the prior's structure. This term strongly prefers highly complex, flexible models. If we allow the Matérn length-scales ($\ell_K$ and $\ell_T$) to shrink toward zero, the covariance between distinct points vanishes, $K_y$ approaches a scaled identity matrix, and the GP essentially memorises the training data. Consequently, the model loses all of its extrapolation capabilities. A model that perfectly interpolates every noisy observation will drive this data fit penalty toward zero, pushing the LML higher.

	\item \textbf{The Complexity Penalty (Log-Determinant):} The term $-\frac{1}{2}\log|K_y|$ acts as an inherent regularisation mechanism. When the length-scales are small, the points one unit apart become weakly correlated. Hence, the kernel matrix is nearly diagonal, changing the log-determinant term. In other words, when the model allows the function to vary widely from point to point, the penalty becomes more negative, reducing the marginal likelihood.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The Origin of Weak Identifiability (Flat Ridges):}
In an ideal scenario, the competition between the Data Fit and the Complexity penalty results in a convex, unimodal peak in the LML landscape. In this case, the algorithm can easily identify a single optimal parameter set $\hat{\theta}$. On the other hand, financial data exhibit characteristic noise and friction (e.g., bid-ask spread noise). Where the LML may exhibit weak identifiability when different hyperparameter combinations yield nearly the same covariance matrix $K_\theta$. This is because the option market data can be successfully interpolated using multiple combinations of strike and maturity length-scales (e.g.,\TODO{find a convincing but complex example}). Mathematically, at a local optimum $\hat{\theta}$, the Hessian of the negative LML measures local curvature. If the Hessian has a very small eigenvalue, then the LML changes slowly in the corresponding direction. Geometrically, this produces a plateau or flat ridges with weakly identifiable hyperparameters.

\paragraph{Standard Software Implementation and the Mathematical Failure of Quasi-Newton Optimisation:} Standard Gaussian Process libraries (such as scikit-learn) navigate this objective landscape using L-BFGS-B (Limited-memory Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno with Bounds), a deterministic Quasi-Newton optimisation algorithm. 

Let $(\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \log p(\mathbf{y} | X, \theta)$. L-BFGS-B seeks to maximise the LML by iteratively solving a local quadratic approximation centred at the current hyperparameter estimate $\theta_k$:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta_k + p) \approx \mathcal{L}(\theta_k) + \nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta_k)^\top p + \frac{1}{2} p^\top \mathcal{H}_k p,$$

where $\mathcal{H}_k = \nabla^2 \mathcal{L}(\theta_k)$ is the Hessian. The Taylor expansion is used because the LML is a complicated nonlinear function of $\theta$ with no closed-form optimal step, but locally around $\theta_k$, any smooth function looks quadratic, as Taylor's theorem guarantees.

However, $\mathcal{H}_k$ is never computed exactly. Computing it analytically requires differentiating through the kernel matrix, involving terms of the form $\operatorname{tr}\!\left(K_y^{-1} \frac{\partial K_y}{\partial \theta_i}\right)$, which are expensive when $N$ is large. Moreover, the Hessian can become nearly singular, for instance, when hyperparameters are weakly identifiable or approach a boundary, making direct inversion numerically unstable. L-BFGS-B addresses both issues by building a low-rank approximation $B_k^{-1} \approx \mathcal{H}_k^{-1}$ from the last $m$ gradient difference pairs $(s_j, r_j)$ and determining the search direction as $p_k = -B_k^{-1} \nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta_k)$.

The Quasi-Newton approach is computationally efficient in strictly convex regimes, but it can fail in the presence of weak invertibility. To prove this, we must decompose the curvature matrix ($\mathcal{H}$) into its principal directions and magnitudes. We rewrite the Hessian as a sum of eigen-directions, each with an eigenvalue attached. The Hessian $\mathcal{H}$ at a point on a plateau admits an eigenpair ($\lambda_{ridge},v_{ridge}$) with $|\lambda_{ridge}| \approx 0$, where $v_{ridge}$ goes along the tangent direction of the simple curvature, causing the condition number to diverge:

$$\kappa(\mathcal{H}) = \frac{|\lambda_{max}|}{|\lambda_{min}|} \to \infty$$

The divergence breaks the algorithm's mechanics in two ways. 

First, in the search direction update $p_k = -\mathcal{H}^{-1} \nabla \mathcal{L}$, the inverse eigenvalue $1/\lambda_{min} \to \infty$ artificially amplifies infinitesimal numerical noise in the gradient along the ridge direction. The optimiser overreacts to very small numerical errors in the direction of the plateau. To satisfy the Wolfe conditions and prevent the step size from exploding, the line search algorithm compensates by heavily truncating the step length $\alpha_k$, even though the actual signal is basically flat. The algorithm ends up making tiny moves or getting stuck and thinks it has converged.

Second, the standard L-BFGS-B termination uses a stopping rule; the criterion triggers when the maximum component of the projected gradient falls below a predefined tolerance $\epsilon$:$$\max_i |(\text{proj} \nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta_k))_i| < \epsilon$$ On a flat ridge, the algorithm thinks it has converged because the gradient is below that threshold. In reality, the surface is nearly flat along that direction. As the directional derivative vanishes ($\nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta) \cdot v_{ridge} \approx 0$), the optimiser mistakenly interprets this flat gradient as evidence of a true stationary point. It prematurely terminates the line search, declaring convergence at a suboptimal coordinate $\hat{\theta}_{local}$ rather than traversing the ridge and actually looking for a better one. As will be empirically demonstrated in Chapter 4, this structural pathology causes point-estimated models to become highly sensitive to random initialisations, trapping them in local optima and yielding unpredictable, financially unstable volatility surfaces.

\section{Restart Instability}
The ultimate choice of hyperparameters is heavily dependent on the initial position of the optimiser. The issue of the optimiser's sensitivity to initialisation coordinates is made especially clear when navigating a flat ridge, as in Figure \ref{fig:topology-2026-06-12}. The standard practice in Gaussian Process regression is to run the L-BFGS-B algorithm from multiple random starting points (restarts) and select the hyperparameter configuration that yields the highest LML. 

During the empirical evaluation for 2026-06-11, the pipeline made 20 restarts. The process repeatedly triggered the \texttt{ConvergenceWarning} flag, explicitly indicating that the optimal value was outside the specified lower length-scale bounds. Importantly, the restarts did not converge to a single consensus estimate. Additionally, the standard deviation of the final LML values across the restarts was high at 90.02.

To understand why the initialisation coordinate carries such importance for the resulting estimate, we must mathematically break down the mechanics of the L-BFGS-B algorithm in the presence of a non-convex plateau. Let $\theta_0^{(1)}$ and $\theta_0^{(2)}$ be two distinct random initialisation vectors. The optimiser defines a descent direction $p_k$ and relies on the strong Wolfe conditions to dictate the step length $\alpha_k$:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L}(\theta_k + \alpha_k p_k) &\leq \mathcal{L}(\theta_k) + c_1 \alpha_k \nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta_k)^\top p_k \quad \text{(Sufficient Decrease)} \\
    |\nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta_k + \alpha_k p_k)^\top p_k| &\leq c_2 |\nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta_k)^\top p_k| \quad \text{(Curvature Condition)} \label{eq:curvature_condition}
\end{align}
where $0 < c_1 < c_2 < 1$. When the initialisation point $\theta_0^{(1)}$ lands on one side of the plateau, the algorithm rapidly descends the steep walls of the objective function, successfully satisfying the Wolfe conditions. However, the moment the trajectory reaches the flat ridge, the gradient magnitude collapses ($\|\nabla \mathcal{L}\| \to 0$) and the algorithm declares convergence. 

At this point, the curvature condition (Equation \ref{eq:curvature_condition}) is the final stage in which the decision is made about whether to keep the step size $\alpha_k$. When the algorithm descends into the valley, any step size yields a negligible change in the direction derivative $\nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta_k)$. In other words, neither the sufficient decrease nor the curvature conditions will be satisfied, leading the algorithm to converge falsely. Consequently, the final estimate $\hat{\theta}^{(1)}$ is entirely dependent on the specific location where the trajectory of $\theta_0^{(1)}$ first intercepted the ridge. Similarly, a different initialisation coordinate $\theta_0^{(2)}$ will intercept the plateau at a different location and terminate there. The resulting estimate of $\theta_0^{(n)}$ will yield different parameters; none of them will be the optimal true maximum likelihood estimate, but rather just a random artefact of the optimiser's starting position.

\chapter{Bayesian Hyperparameter Inference via HMC}

\section{From Optimisation to Integration}
In the previous chapter, we demonstrated that the topology of the log-marginal space for the financial volatility surfaces is quite ill-posed and features weak hyperparameter identifiability. This is because deterministic optimisation algorithms (e.g., L-BFGS-B) seek to maximise this objective function without accounting for the fact that the hyperparameter space cannot be collapsed to a single point estimate, which is not a definitive optimum. On the other hand, it is an arbitrary coordinate dictated by the optimiser's initialisation and line-search termination criteria. 

To address this structural fragility, this thesis examines the problem through the lens of integration rather than optimisation. Instead of attempting to find a single optimal estimate, we will adopt a fully Bayesian framework. This seeks to keep the full probability distribution over what those potential parameters could be, given the data. By applying Bayes' theorem to the GP hyperparameters, we define the posterior distribution as:

\begin{equation}
p(\theta \mid \mathbf{y}, X) = \frac{p(\mathbf{y} \mid X, \theta) p(\theta)}{\int p(\mathbf{y} \mid X, \theta^\prime) p(\theta^\prime) d\theta^\prime}
\label{eq:bayesian_theorem}
\end{equation}

Where $p(\mathbf{y} \mid X, \theta)$ is the exact marginal likelihood defined in Chapter 3, and $p(\theta)$ is a joint prior distribution placed over the hyperparameters (such as the Matérn length-scales and noise variance) to constrain them to strictly positive, financially sensible bounds.

The advantage of this shift can be observed through forecasting. The standard point estimation methods rely on the rigid assumption that $\theta^*$ represents the absolute truth for predictions at unseen test inputs. Resulting in the condition predictive distribution $p(\mathbf{f}_* \mid X_*, X, \mathbf{y}, \theta^*)$. In contrast, the fully Bayesian predictive distribution marginalises out the hyperparameter uncertainty entirely by integrating the conditional predictions over the full posterior:

$$p(\mathbf{f}_* \mid X_*, X, \mathbf{y}) = \int p(\mathbf{f}_* \mid X_*, X, \mathbf{y}, \theta) p(\theta \mid \mathbf{y}, X) d\theta$$

In practical terms, this integral represents a continuous ensemble of Gaussian Processes. Rather than just picking one of them, which can lead to unstable extrapolation if the optimiser lands on a bad spot. The Bayesian integral avoids this by hedging across all plausible explanations of the data. More specifically, the integral averages the predictive surfaces over all possible hyperparameter combinations, weighting them by their posterior probabilities. This approach handles cases where the data weakly identify whether a local market movement is driven by a short strike length scale ($\ell_K$) or elevated signal variance ($\sigma_f^2$); the Bayesian predictive surface hedges between these competing scenarios. This structural averaging naturally avoids extreme localised extrapolations. Consequently, lowering the out-of-sample pricing error.

It is important to note that computing the exact Bayesian predictive distribution is practically impossible for a Gaussian Process regression. The primary reason for that is the denominator in the \eqref{eq:bayesian_theorem}, which lacks a closed-form analytical solution due to the highly nonlinear relationship between the hyperparameters and the inverted Matérn covariance matrix. As a consequence, this integral cannot be computed directly. Instead, we must approximate it using stochastic numerical methods. Hence, justifying the transition of analytical calculus to Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulation.

\section{Markov Chain Monte Carlo}
Having established that the computation of the Bayesian predictive integral exactly is analytically infeasible, we take the approach of Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods to construct an approximation of the posterior. The objective is to generate a range of dependent samples $\{\theta^{(1)}, \theta^{(2)}, \dots, \theta^{(S)}\}$ such that, as $S \to \infty$, the empirical distribution converges to the exact target prior $p(\theta \mid \mathbf{y}, X)$.

The standard approach to this approximation problem is the Random Walk Metropolis (RWM) algorithm. Which takes the current parameter value and at each iteration step $s$, the algorithm suggests a nearby candidate $\theta^\prime$ by adding isotropic Gaussian noise:

$$\theta^\prime \sim \mathcal{N}(\theta^{(s)}, \Sigma_{prop})$$


where $\Sigma_{prop} = \sigma^2 I$ is the isotropic variance and controls the step size. The proposal is then accepted or rejected based on the Metropolis acceptance probability $\alpha$: (\TODO{should I explain how the Metropolis sampling works?})

$$\alpha = \min\left(1, \frac{p(\theta^\prime \mid \mathbf{y}, X)}{p(\theta^{(s)} \mid \mathbf{y}, X)}\right)$$

The RWM algorithm is guaranteed to asymptotically converge to the true posterior in the limit of infinite iterations, but becomes very inefficient in $d$-dimensional space. Its mixing time increases substantially because, by the \textbf{concentration of measure} principle, most of the posterior mass lies in a thin \textbf{typical set} rather than near the mode. Hence, isotropic proposals either have low acceptance rates or slow convergence.

More specifically, the RWM relies on a gradient-free proposal distribution, in turn leading to a structural trade-off between the step size $\sigma$ and the acceptance rate $\alpha$. If the step size $\sigma$ is chosen to be small, the candidate state remains within the typical set ($\alpha \approx 1$), and the exploration degrades into a diffusive random walk. After $N$ steps, the distance scales like $\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N})$, so the algorithm takes a long time to explore the full ridge geometry.

Conversely, if the steps taken by the algorithm are large. The isotropic candidate state will often be outside the narrow bounds of the typical set. Where the target density $p(\theta^\prime \mid \mathbf{y}, X) \to 0$. Consequently, the acceptance probability $\alpha$ plummets, thereby paralysing the chain. Mathematically, for the acceptance probability ratio $\alpha$ to be nonzero, the size step must be scaled down by a factor of $\mathcal{O}(d^{-1/2})$ at the cost of stagnating the exploration of the surface in higher dimensions.

To avoid this diffusive, random-walk behaviour, we replace the isotropic, gradient-free candidate state model with a gradient-informed MCMC algorithm based on $\nabla_\theta \log p(\theta \mid \mathbf{y}, X)$. Which will actively steer the Markov chain along the contours of the typical set.

\section{Hamiltonian Monte Carlo}
A practical solution to the previous problem is the Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC), which reformulates the statistical sampling problem as a deterministic simulation of classical mechanics. The algorithm is able to explore the high-dimensional typical set by introducing an auxiliary momentum variable and using information from the gradient of the target density to construct a vector field. 

\subsection{Phase Space and the Hamiltonian}

Let the Gaussian Process hyperparameter vector $\theta$ map to a position vector $q \in \mathbb{R}^d$. To model this as a physical system, we introduce a fictitious, independent momentum vector $p \in \mathbb{R}^d$ representing an additional random push. The original parameter space is thus expanded into a $2d$-dimensional \textbf{phase space} $(q, p)$. This approach allows us to treat the hyperparameters as a point moving in the mathematical space. Essentially, we are artificially expanding the space so the sampling method can behave more like a physical system. Intuitively, we are applying a concept that serves as a computational helper, because we do not want the momentum variable to alter the target posterior over the original parameters. We explicitly define the joint distribution of the target variables and the auxiliary momentum as statistically independent: $p(q, p) = p(q)p(p)$. Where the Hamiltonian $H(q, p)$ is the energy of this artificial system. In statistical mechanics, the canonical distribution relates the joint density to the energy function $H(q, p)$:

$$p(q, p) = \frac{1}{Z} \exp(-H(q, p))$$

where $Z$ is the partition function. Hence, the joint distribution can be defined up to a constant. Because we defined the position and momentum to be independent, the Hamiltonian can be separated into Potential Energy $U(q)$ and Kinetic Energy $K(p)$:

$$H(q, p) = U(q) + K(p)$$

The potential energy $U(q)$ is defined as the negative logarithm of the unnormalized target posterior density under the Gaussian Process framework:

$$U(q) = -\log p(\mathbf{y} \mid X, q) - \log p(q)$$

The first term corresponds to the negative log-marginal likelihood of the Matérn $5/2$ covariance structure that we defined in Chapter 3. The second term, $-\log p(q)$, accounts for the prior of the hyperparameters. Furthermore, the strictly positive Matérn parameters $\theta = \{\ell_K, \ell_T, \sigma_f, \sigma_n\}$ are mapped to the real line $q = \log(\theta)$ to ensure that the HMC particle traverses a smooth plane and doesn't diverge into numerical singularities during integration.

\subsection{Hamiltonian Dynamics}
The evolution of the system through phase space follows the rules of motion given by Hamilton's equations. They constitute a system of $2d$ coupled first-order ordinary differential equations that tell us how the state evolves over time.


$$\frac{dq_i}{dt} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} = [M^{-1} p]_i$$

$$\frac{dp_i}{dt} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i} = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial q_i}$$

In practical terms, the first equation says that the position changes at a rate determined by the momentum. Hence, if momentum is larger in some direction, the parameter moves faster in that direction. While the other equation describes how the momentum changes according to the negative slope of the potential energy landscape. So, as the negative log-posterior increases, the particle drifts in some direction; the negative gradient pushes the particle back towards more probable regions, allowing it to rapidly traverse the flat ridges rather than wandering blindly through phase space.

\subsection{Theoretical Guarantees: Conservation, Volume, and Reversibility}

In order for the Hamiltonian dynamics to be valid as a mechanism for a Markov chain, the continuous-time evolution must satisfy three strict conditions: energy conservation, volume preservation, and time reversibility. In other words, for the HMC to efficiently explore the surface's geometry, it must obey the three laws so it doesn't change the posterior distribution it samples from.

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Energy Conservation}
The energy conservation rule keeps the Hamiltonian invariant, conserving energy over time. \textbf{Proof:} By applying the multivariate chain rule and substituting Hamilton's equations, the time derivative becomes $H$:

\begin{equation}
\frac{dH}{dt} = \sum_{i=1}^d \left( \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i} \frac{dq_i}{dt} + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} \frac{dp_i}{dt} \right)
\label{energy_conservation}
\end{equation}

$$\frac{dH}{dt} = \sum_{i=1}^d \left( \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i} \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i} \right) = 0$$


Because $\frac{dH}{dt} = 0$, the particle glides along isocontours of the joint density $p(q, p)$. Hence, the trajectory does not move toward higher or lower Hamiltonian values and remains at the same probability level rather than exploring them. In practice, we cannot integrate Hamilton's equations analytically, so we approximate the continuous dynamics using the Leapfrog method reviewed in Chapter 5.4.

\section{Data Ingestion and Preprocessing}

The empirical analysis utilises European-style option chains on the S\&P 500 Index (SPX). Due to the sheer density of the SPX options market, evaluating the entire continuous surface simultaneously is computationally expensive and mathematically unnecessary for the sake of hyperparameter inference. Therefore, the data ingestion pipeline is designed to construct a representative discrete term structure.

The market data was extracted via the Yahoo Finance API. A number of regimes were selected to test the HMC sampler, ranging from high-distress volatility to sustained-volatility regimes. The pipeline extracts a stratified cross-section of maturities, specifically targeting near-term (2 weeks), medium-term (5 and 12 weeks), and long-term (20 weeks) expirations. This provides the Gaussian Process with enough data to model a vast topological space that captures both steep, convex skew of short-dated options and the flatter, asymptotic behaviour of long-dated contracts. 

For each maturity, the raw option chains are filtered using a strict preprocessing protocol to remove erroneous quotes and microstructure noise that will interfere with the construction of the Log-Marginal Likelihood landscape:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Liquidity and Validity Constraints:} 
    The market exhibits microstructure noise for various reasons, including, but not limited to, stale quotes and widening bid-ask spreads for OTM options. The noise changes the covariance matrix by adding a noise component (equation \ref{noise_covariance_matrix}). Thereby, altering conditioning, effective weighting, and hyperparameter estimation. To prevent instability, we strictly filter the dataset. Any option with a trading volume of zero, or missing bid/ask quotes ($Bid \le 0$ or $Ask \le 0$), is discarded. Furthermore, quotes with extreme implied volatilities ($\sigma_{\text{imp}} \le 0.01$ or $\sigma_{\text{imp}} \ge 2.5$) are removed, as these often are pricing errors or highly illiquid strikes.
    \item \textbf{Coordinate Transformation:} 
    A more natural coordinate system than the regular $(K,T)$ is the $k = \log(K/S_0)$. The log-moneyness represents a relative coordinate space that puts the strike price $K$ into perspective with respect to the spot price $S_0$. To ensure the Matérn kernel operates on a stable, scale-invariant domain, the strikes are mapped to log-moneyness $k = \log(K/S_0)$. Simultaneously, the expiration dates are converted into an annualised time-to-maturity metric $T$.
    \item \textbf{Target Variable Construction:} 
    As mentioned in Section \ref{subsec:static-arbitrage}, the target variable for the Gaussian Process is not the raw implied volatility, but rather the total implied variance. Following Gatheral's SVI framework, the filtered implied volatilities are squared and scaled by maturity: $w(k,T) = \sigma_{\text{imp}}^2(k,T) \, T$. This ensures the calendar arbitrage monotonicity constraints detailed in Section \ref{subsubsec:calendar-arbitrage} can be accurately evaluated.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
To measure the performance of the competing inference methodologies, we must separate the evaluation framework into global predictive accuracy and local financial consistency. As established in Section \ref{subsec:static-arbitrage}, a mathematically smooth interpolant is financially invalid if it permits arbitrage. Therefore, the models are evaluated across the following three numerical metrics:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Out-of-Sample Predictive Accuracy (RMSE):}
	The Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) measures the out-of-sample accuracy to determine the global fit of the model, which is strictly evaluated on the testing set. Let $N_{\text{test}}$ be the number of unseen option quotes, $y_i$ be the observed total implied variance, and $\hat{y}_i$ be the model's predictive mean. The out-of-sample RMSE is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \text{RMSE}_{\text{OOS}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N_{\text{test}}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{test}}} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}
\end{equation}

This metric penalises models that overfit the training data's microstructural noise by measuring their generalisation error on unseen coordinates.

	\item \textbf{Calendar Arbitrage Violations:}
	While Section \ref{subsec:static-arbitrage} defines the calendar arbitrage as a continuous monotonicity condition ($\partial w / \partial T \geq 0$), the numerical implementation is done using forward finite differences. Let $\mathbf{T} = \{T_1, T_2, \dots, T_m\}$ represent the sorted unique maturities. For each strike coordinate, the discrete derivative is computed and formally recorded as calendar arbitrage. Later summed whenever the discrete variance increment is negative:
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{j=1}^{m-1} \mathbb{I} \left( \frac{w(k, T_{j+1}) - w(k, T_j)}{T_{j+1} - T_j} < 0 \right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function.

	\item \textbf{Butterfly Arbitrage Violations:}
	Similarly, the theoretical condition for the absence of butterfly arbitrage requires the risk-neutral probability density to remain non-negative, corresponding to a strictly convex call price curve ($\partial^2 C / \partial K^2 \geq 0$). To calculate it, we invert the predictive surface $w(k, T)$ to obtain European call prices $C(K, T)$ using the standard Black-Scholes pricing formula because the GP interpolates in total variance space. We evaluate the second derivative using a numerical approximation at the midpoint. Given a sorted grid of unique strike prices $\mathbf{K} = \{K_1, K_2, \dots, K_p\}$, let $\Delta C_i = C(K_{i+1}, T) - C(K_i, T)$ and $\Delta K_i = K_{i+1} - K_i$. The evaluation becomes:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial K^2} \approx \frac{\frac{\Delta C_{i+1}}{\Delta K_{i+1}} - \frac{\Delta C_i}{\Delta K_i}}{\Delta K_i}
\end{equation}

The pipeline aggregates total butterfly violations by applying this finite-difference array operation and counting every instance in which the discrete second derivative falls below zero across all evaluated maturities.
\end{enumerate}

Previously, we established the theoretical foundations of deterministic point estimation in Chapter 4 and formalised the Bayesian Hamiltonian Monte Carlo methodology in Chapter 5. This chapter will present the empirical findings from the evaluation conducted across the varying market regimes defined in Section \ref{sec:market-regimes}.

\section{Predictive Accuracy: The Global Fit}

The primary objective of any volatility surface interpolant is to accurately generalise to new market coordinates. The out-of-sample Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) is used to gauge the performance of this generalisation. 

As established in Chapter 4, the deterministic optimiser exhibits weak identifiability on flat, non-convex ridges in the LML topological landscapes. The L-BFGS-B algorithm often stops prematurely at an arbitrary coordinate, subject to its initialisation point. Thereby, constraining the predictions of the GP and leads to severe overfitting of the model. Conversely, the Bayesian HMC framework integrates over the entire weakly identifiable ridge. By drawing samples from the full posterior distribution and generating a predictive mean that is an expectation over the hyperparameter space, the HMC approach acts as a structural regulariser. 

The observed results across different market regimes explicitly validate this hypothesis, particularly during heavy market stress and turmoil. The biggest error reduction occurred during the high-momentum expansion regime of early 2024. During this period, the deterministic optimiser was unable to generalise the surface correctly, resulting in a large OOT RMSE. As detailed in Appendix C, on 24 January 2024, the Point-Estimated GP yielded a massive out-of-sample RMSE of $0.1259$ ($12.59\%$). Similarly, on 9 February 2024, the deterministic error spiked to $0.1861$ ($18.61\%$).

In stark contrast, the Bayesian HMC GP was able to contain the flat topological surface during these exact same periods. By marginalising over the hyperparameter uncertainty, the HMC framework reduced the generalisation error on 24 January 2024 to just $0.0380$ ($3.80\%$). A monumental $\mathbf{\sim70\%}$ reduction in predictive error compared to the deterministic baseline. On 9 February, the HMC error was at $0.0691$, representing a $\mathbf{\sim62\%}$ improvement against the L-BFGS-B algorithm.

\section{Visualising the Posterior Diagnostics}
To understand how the Hamiltonian Monte Carlo sampler achieved the said error reductions, we must dig deeper and examine the empirical posterior distributions of the hyperparameter. As mentioned in Chapter 4, the LML space for financial volatility surfaces often features weakly identifiable, flat ridges. Instead of being perfectly convex, the optima is a plateau. 

Figure \ref{fig:posterior_2020_03_03} illustrates the posterior distributions for the strike ($\ell_K$) and maturity ($\ell_T$) length-scales on 3 March 2020, a period of extreme market distress.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Figures/hmc_posterior_2020-03-03.png}
    \caption{Posterior distributions of the Matérn length-scales during the High Volatility Shock (3 March 2020). Note the distinct bimodal structure at the boundaries, indicative of navigation along a flat LML ridge.}
    \label{fig:posterior_2020_03_03}
\end{figure}

Rather than forming a standard, unimodal Gaussian curve, the posterior exhibits a rigid bimodal structure, accumulating heavily at the boundaries of the typical set. This is a direct physical manifestation of the Hamiltonian dynamics interacting with the flat ridge topology mapped in Section 4.1. As the simulated particle traverses the phase space, the gradient of the potential energy along the ridge approaches zero ($\nabla U(q) \approx 0$). When reversing, the particle spends some time at the boundary position, leading the HMC Leapfrog integrator to sum over those coordinates more than at the bottom of the ridge. Without a gradient to continuously redirect the momentum, the HMC particle slides along the ridge until it intercepts the bounds of the weakly identifiable region, accumulating sample mass at the extremities before reversing direction. 

While a deterministic optimiser prematurely terminates upon encountering these flat gradients, causing the restart instability documented in Section 4.2, the HMC algorithm successfully maps the entire ridge. Furthermore, the empirical data reveal that the location of this ridge is highly non-stationary across market regimes. During the high-momentum regime in early 2024, the boundary constraints shifted dynamically. The posterior mass for $\log(\ell_K)$ migrated from the high-correlation range of $[2.3, 3.1]$ observed in 2020, down to a highly localised range of $[0.6, 0.7]$. 

By using structural averaging, the model can weed out extreme behaviours of specific length scales. In other words, it effectively hedges between the mathematical extremes of a weakly identifiable region. This prevents the kernel from memorising microstructural noise or making the surface too rigid.

\section{Financial Consistency and the Arbitrage Trade-off}
\label{sec:consistency_tradeoff}

While the out-of-sample RMSE measures how well the model extrapolates across the macroscopic surface, it does not guarantee economic validity. As mentioned in Section 2.2, a mathematically smooth interpolant is financially inadmissible if it permits arbitrage. Both frameworks were evaluated for financial consistency with respect to the calendar and butterfly arbitrage constraints defined in Section 2.2.1. 

The empirical results, aggregated in Appendix C, reveal the mostly consistent structural flaw in the deterministic point-estimation framework across regimes. By aggressively maximising the log-marginal likelihood, the L-BFGS-B optimiser frequently overfits the microstructural noise of the bid-ask spreads. This is especially evident during the 2024 regime, mentioned in Section 7.1. As the algorithm tries to overfit the noisy data, it creates infinitesimal "kinks" in the predictive surface. When inverted to call prices, these kinks violate the strict convexity condition ($\partial^2 C / \partial K^2 \ge 0$) required by the Breeden-Litzenberger theorem, resulting in negative risk-neutral probabilities. 

An analysis of the 27 unique historical trading days (excluding duplicate API dates) reveals that the Point-Estimated GP generated butterfly arbitrage violations on $\mathbf{100\%}$ of the evaluated dates. More critically, the model regularly suffered from simultaneous, systemic collapses of both static arbitrage conditions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Across the four market regimes, the deterministic point estimator simultaneously violated \emph{both} calendar and butterfly constraints on $37.0\%$ of the evaluated days.
    \item During periods of extreme volatility and momentum (March 2020 and Q1 2024), this joint-failure rate escalated to $66.7\%$ (8 out of 12 unique high-stress days).
    \item Within the High-Momentum Expansion regime of early 2024, the dual-violation rate peaked at a remarkable $85.7\%$ (6 out of 7 unique days), with simultaneous violations spiking as high as 175 calendar and 266 butterfly errors on 18 January 2024.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Accuracy-Consistency Trade-off}
\label{sec:accuracy_consistency_tradeoff}

During highly liquid, low-volatility market environments, the deterministic point estimator frequently achieves a nominally superior out-of-sample RMSE compared to the Bayesian ensemble. However, this performance can be deceiving if we do not consider the overall picture.

\subsection{The Illusion of Deterministic Superiority}

To understand the mechanics of this illusion, we must examine the behaviour of the quasi-Newton optimiser on a highly identifiable Log-Marginal Likelihood (LML) landscape. During a steady, rate-shock bear market of September 2022, the volatility surface is generally smooth and structurally stable. Because the broader shape of the surface is simple, the residual data is largely dominated by microstructural market friction and not necessarily by economic patterns. In which case, the held-out points may contain very similar quote noise, so a model that partly memorises that noise can still score a very low RMSE but be economically incorrect.

\subsection{Empirical Case Studies: The 2022 Grinding Bear Regime}

To explicitly outline this behaviour of the deterministic point estimator, we will consider the SPX options market on \textbf{16 September 2022} and \textbf{21 September 2022} for empirical evaluation. On both dates, the out-of-sample RMSEs were lower than their Bayesian counterparts, a direct result of the point estimator successfully locking onto the exact coordinates of the noisy training data.

\chapter{Conclusion}
\section{The Pathology of Point Estimation}

This dissertation set out to resolve the structural instability present in non-parametric volatility surface models, specifically addressing the failure of the deterministic point estimation approach to Gaussian Processes during market turmoil. The core mathematical discovery of this research is that Log-Marginal Likelihood (LML) for options is often non-convex, featuring a weakly identifiable hyperparameter space. This directly undermines the standard assumption that a single, optimal set of hyperparameters can be isolated from the market data.

Most importantly, this topological mapping reveals a structural flaw, not in the deterministic L-BFGS-B optimiser, but within the Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) objective it is instructed to solve. The L-BFGS-B algorithm is a highly efficient quasi-Newton method; it mathematically succeeds in finding the exact coordinates that maximise the probability of the observed training data. However, in the context of chaotic financial markets, the idea of absolute optimum is a mathematical trap. The absolute optimum corresponds to shorter length scales, where the LML score is highest because it aggressively memorises in-sample microstructural friction, such as bid-ask spreads and asynchronous tick sizes. Consequently, this achieves a nominally lower OOS RMSE in certain regimes.

Therefore, by restricting the model to collapse its hyperparameter uncertainty into a single noise-fitting coordinate on a flat ridge, point estimation transforms the Gaussian Process from generalisable, out-of-sample forecasting into a highly brittle, overfitted function. Directly contradicting the fundamental goal of MLE.

\section{The Accuracy-Consistency Trade-off}

The consequence of this overfitting introduces a dangerous paradigm into quantitative trading: the financial illusion of predictive accuracy. A low generalisation error becomes meaningless unless accompanied by strict arbitrage constraints.

The empirical findings of this research highlight the instability of the deterministic point estimation approach under these economic constraints. Across the evaluated historical data, the point estimator achieved a 100\% butterfly arbitrage failure rate. Additionally, during the aggressive momentum expansion of Q1 2024, this instability escalated into an 85.7\% systemic dual-violation rate, simultaneously failing both calendar and butterfly conditions. Thereby supporting the claim that the pursuit of the absolute maximum likelihood mathematically guaranteed negative risk-neutral probabilities.

Conversely, the Bayesian framework adopts a role of a structural regulariser; the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) integrates across the entire weakly identifiable posterior ridge, generating an ensemble of predictive surfaces. By averaging them, the fatal structural flaw of the point estimator is resolved. Furthermore, by replacing point estimation with Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) inference, the chaotic microstructural noise cancels itself out due to the marginalisation process of the framework.

Ultimately, this regularisation exposes an accuracy-consistency trade-off between out-of-sample error and financial validity. This compromise is especially noticeable during the highly liquid, low-volatility regimes such as September 2022. The Bayesian model accepts a small penalty in generalisation error compared to the deterministic optimiser. However, this marginal reduction in accuracy is the mandatory price for an economically admissible surface, which guarantees the eradication of structural arbitrage.

\section{Computational Limitations and Future Work}

While the Bayesian Hamiltonian Monte Carlo framework decisively outperforms the deterministic point estimation approach in preserving economic admissibility, its deployment and upkeep during live trading sessions are heavily constrained by computational complexity. Although the time complexity of both algorithms is $\mathcal{O}(N^3)$, the fundamental mathematical bottleneck is the computationally expensive Cholesky decomposition of the covariance matrix. The paramount difference between them comes from the constant factor, which represents the number of times the algorithms have to execute this expensive $\mathcal{O}(N^3)$ decomposition. Based on the hyperparameter configurations used in this study, evaluating the full posterior via Hamiltonian Monte Carlo requires roughly 1,000 times more computational overhead than deterministic point estimation.

It is reasonable to assert that the current HMC implementation is most optimally suited for end-of-day settlement pricing, risk management, and portfolio margin calculations. In this scenario, the extreme accuracy and strictly arbitrage-free surfaces are important, but execution latency is a secondary concern. Furthermore, the current computational complexity of the NUTS sampler is expensive and impractical for High Frequency Trading (HFT), where volatility surfaces must be updated on sub-millisecond timescales.

The logical progression for future quantitative research is to bridge this computational gap by exploring Variational Inference (VI) or Sparse Gaussian Processes. By framing Bayesian inference as an optimisation problem rather than a stochastic sampling problem, VI has the potential to approximate the true Bayesian posterior in a fraction of the time. Successfully implementing a Variational Gaussian Process could theoretically maintain the powerful regularising benefits of hyperparameter ensembling while achieving the near-deterministic execution speeds required for high-frequency trading floors.
